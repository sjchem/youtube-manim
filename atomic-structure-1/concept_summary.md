# Science and teaching notes

The film asks how scientists inferred atomic structure before images of
individual atoms were available. It follows model, prediction, experiment,
surprise and revision. It does not imply that atomic imaging is impossible today.

| Picture | What earns its place | Where it needs revision |
|---|---|---|
| Dalton's indivisible atom | Definite chemical proportions | Atoms contain smaller charged constituents |
| Thomson's distributed positive charge | Balances negative electrons | Cannot explain the observed large-angle scattering rate |
| Rutherford's nucleus | Concentrates charge and nearly all mass | Classical electron orbits radiate and collapse |
| Bohr's stationary energies | Main hydrogen lines and stable states within the model | Circular paths are not the modern description; multi-electron atoms need more |

## Analogy boundaries

The dark-room balls explain inference from paths. Actual alpha particles turn
under Coulomb repulsion; they need not hit the nuclear surface. Both misses and
bounces carry evidence. The detector ring is a diagram of possible detector
positions, not a claim that the original apparatus counted every angle at once.
The selected tracks exaggerate the frequency of rare events. The familiar
one-in-twenty-thousand figure is a textbook example, not a universal probability.

A nucleus enlarged to cricket-ball size corresponds to an atom extending several
kilometres. The scene uses `AtomicScale(atom_radius=1.4e-10)` with a nuclear radius
of `1e-15 m` and a ball radius of `0.036 m`: `0.036 × 140000 / 1000 = 5.04 km`.
This illustrative choice is consistent with the approximate “about 5 km” wording.
Using `1e-10 m` instead gives 3.6 km. Neither figure describes every atom, and
atomic boundaries are not hard walls. The overview compresses scale and enlarges
the central marker for visibility. Electrons and fields occupy the volume outside
the nucleus; electrical interactions and quantum rules matter for solidity.

The staircase represents allowed stationary energies of a bound electron.
Transition arrows indicate energy changes, not observed spatial trajectories.
Bohr postulated nonradiating stationary states; it is not a classical derivation
of stability. The modern explanation is left for the next video.

## Spectral details

The animation displays four prominent Balmer lines, corresponding to transitions
from levels three through six to level two. Hydrogen has other lines too. The
white-light band is a comparison, not a logical consequence of every continuous
energy model. Atomic motion and environments can shift or split lines, so the
script does not claim that observed line positions never change.

The level-five-to-level-two example is approximately 434 nm. Rounded constants in
this educational calculation are not intended for precision spectroscopy.
Higher frequency means shorter wavelength, not faster light in a vacuum.
The Bohr formula also applies to hydrogen-like one-electron ions with the
appropriate nuclear-charge factor; the rendered ladder here is for hydrogen.

The ending's probability cloud represents possible detections of one electron,
not many electrons or a classical substance smeared around a circular path.
Electrons retain particle-like detection as well as wave-like behaviour.
