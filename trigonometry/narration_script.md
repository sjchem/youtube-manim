A heartbeat, a pendulum, and sound all repeat. Their physical details are different, but each one returns through the same pattern again and again. When we graph that repetition through time, a familiar shape appears: a wave. Follow the wave backward and we discover circular motion. Freeze that circle at one instant and a triangle appears. Follow the triangle’s changing sides and we arrive at trigonometry. It all begins with a turn. ## Scene 02 — Angles, Degrees, and Radians — 1:35–3:05 — Voice Clip 02

That amount of turning is what an angle really measures. We often meet an angle as the corner between two lines, but the corner is only a frozen picture of movement. Fix one ray and rotate the other. A quarter-turn is ninety degrees, a half-turn is one hundred and eighty, three quarters is two hundred and seventy, and a full turn is three hundred and sixty degrees. Six equal sixty-degree sectors fit neatly into the circle, but degrees are only one way to measure a turn. Mathematicians often use a unit connected directly to the circle. Copy the radius and bend its length along the circumference. When the arc is exactly as long as the radius, the angle is one radian, about fifty-seven point three degrees. The circumference is two pi times the radius, so a complete turn contains two pi radius-lengths, or two pi radians. Degrees count familiar slices; radians measure arc using the circle’s own radius. They describe the same rotation: three hundred and sixty degrees equals two pi radians. But a rotating point does more than create an angle. Its horizontal and vertical positions give it an address, so before building our triangle, let us understand the coordinate grid beneath it.

## Scene 03 — Coordinates and Pythagoras — 3:05–6:05 — Voice Clip 03

Two perpendicular number lines turn the empty plane into a map. The horizontal line is the x-axis, and the vertical line is the y-axis. A point’s address is written as an ordered pair: horizontal movement first, vertical movement second. The point three, two means move three units to the right and two units up. Change the signs and the point travels into a different quadrant. In quadrant one, both coordinates are positive. In quadrant two, x is negative while y is positive. In quadrant three, both are negative. In quadrant four, x is positive while y is negative. These signs will later tell us the signs of cosine and sine without a separate rule. Now return the point to three, two and connect it to the origin. Its horizontal and vertical movements form perpendicular sides, and the direct line back to the origin becomes a hypotenuse. Coordinates have quietly created a right triangle. How long is that diagonal? Build a square on each side. A side of three creates an area of nine. A side of four creates an area of sixteen. Together those areas make twenty-five, exactly the area of the square on the side of length five. This is the Pythagorean theorem: for every right triangle, a squared plus b squared equals c squared, where c is the hypotenuse. It is not merely a trick for finding missing lengths. It says that perpendicular motion combines through squares. Move horizontally and vertically, and Pythagoras tells us the direct distance. The same theorem will later become the central identity of trigonometry. For now, notice something else. If we enlarge this triangle, its lengths change, but its shape can remain identical. That unchanging shape is where trigonometric ratios begin.

## Scene 04 — Similar Triangles and Ratios — 6:05–8:25 — Voice Clip 04

Return to the frozen rotating ray and drop a vertical line from its endpoint to the horizontal axis. A right triangle appears. The rotating ray is the hypotenuse. Relative to our chosen angle, the horizontal side is adjacent and the vertical side is opposite. Begin with a friendly three-four-five triangle: opposite is three, adjacent is four, and hypotenuse is five. Instead of looking only at the separate lengths, compare them. Opposite over hypotenuse is three fifths. Adjacent over hypotenuse is four fifths. Opposite over adjacent is three quarters. Now enlarge the triangle. Every side grows by the same scale factor. The new decimal lengths look different, yet each fraction simplifies straight back to its original value. Whenever one side is divided by another, the common scale cancels. Every right triangle with the same acute angle has the same shape, and therefore the same three ratios. We give them names. Sine of theta is opposite divided by hypotenuse. Cosine of theta is adjacent divided by hypotenuse. Tangent of theta is opposite divided by adjacent. Keep the hypotenuse fixed and change only the angle. The vertical side grows while the horizontal side shrinks; sine rises, cosine falls, and tangent responds to both. The values belong to the angle, not to the overall size of the drawing. That is why a calculator can return one sine value without asking how large your triangle is. These three ratios are the core of right-triangle trigonometry. A familiar mnemonic can help us read them quickly, and flipping those three ratios will give us the complete family of six trigonometric functions.

## Scene 05 — SOH–CAH–TOA and the Six Functions — 8:25–11:25 — Voice Clip 05

Label the sides once more: opposite, adjacent, and hypotenuse, always relative to the angle we are using. The phrase SOH–CAH–TOA compresses the three main ratios into three short sounds. SOH means sine is opposite over hypotenuse. CAH means cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse. TOA means tangent is opposite over adjacent. The mnemonic is useful, but the picture is more important. If the chosen angle moves to another corner, opposite and adjacent can exchange roles, while the hypotenuse remains the side across from the right angle. Now place the three ratios on the left. Each fraction has a mirror formed by turning it upside down. Flip sine, opposite over hypotenuse, and we obtain hypotenuse over opposite. That function is cosecant. Flip cosine, adjacent over hypotenuse, and we obtain hypotenuse over adjacent. That is secant. Flip tangent, opposite over adjacent, and we obtain adjacent over opposite. That is cotangent. The six functions therefore come in reciprocal pairs: sine with cosecant, cosine with secant, and tangent with cotangent. They are not six unrelated inventions. Four can be constructed directly from sine and cosine. Tangent equals sine divided by cosine. Cotangent equals cosine divided by sine. Secant is one divided by cosine, and cosecant is one divided by sine. This also explains when a function becomes undefined. If cosine is zero, dividing by it makes tangent and secant undefined. If sine is zero, cosecant and cotangent are undefined. The warning comes from the denominator, not from an arbitrary prohibition. Learn sine and cosine deeply, and the rest of the family follows. To evaluate these functions exactly, rather than approximately with a calculator, we need only two beautifully simple triangles.

## Scene 06 — The Special Angles — 11:25–15:25 — Voice Clip 06

Start with a square whose side length is one. Draw a diagonal from one corner to the opposite corner. The diagonal divides each right angle into two equal forty-five-degree angles, creating two identical right triangles. Both legs have length one. Pythagoras says the diagonal squared is one squared plus one squared, so its length is the square root of two. For either forty-five-degree angle, sine is one over square root two, which is commonly written square root two over two. Cosine has the same value because the two legs are equal, and tangent is one. Nothing was memorized; the values came from the square. Now build an equilateral triangle with every side equal to two. Each angle is sixty degrees. Drop an altitude from the top. It bisects the base into two pieces of length one and splits the top angle into two thirty-degree angles. We now have two thirty–sixty–ninety triangles. The hypotenuse is two and the short leg is one. Pythagoras gives the remaining height as square root three. So the side pattern opposite thirty, sixty, and ninety degrees is one, square root three, two. From this pattern, sine thirty is one half and cosine thirty is square root three over two. For sixty degrees those values exchange places. Tangent thirty is one over square root three, and tangent sixty is square root three. At zero degrees, the unit-circle point will sit at one, zero, so sine is zero and cosine is one. At ninety degrees it sits at zero, one, so sine is one and cosine is zero; tangent is undefined because it would require division by zero. Arrange those results into a table and a pattern emerges. Sine rises from zero through square root one, square root two, square root three, and square root four, all divided by two. Cosine contains the same list in reverse. This pattern is a memory aid, but the square and equilateral triangle are the explanation. Place the angles zero, thirty, forty-five, sixty, and ninety degrees around the first quarter of a circle. Each ray ends at an exact coordinate obtained from those triangles. The next step is not to memorize a larger table. It is to use symmetry. Reflect the first-quarter points through the coordinate axes, keep their coordinate sizes, and change only the appropriate signs. One small collection of exact triangles is enough to populate the entire unit circle.

## Scene 07 — The Unit Circle Takes Over — 15:25–17:30 — Voice Clip 07

Now scale a right triangle until its hypotenuse is exactly one. Our three-four-five triangle becomes three fifths, four fifths, and one. Dividing by a hypotenuse of one changes nothing, so the opposite side itself is sine theta, and the adjacent side itself is cosine theta. The hypotenuse is also a radius. Keep its starting point fixed and let it rotate. Its endpoint traces a circle of radius one: the unit circle. At every position, the point’s horizontal coordinate is cosine theta and its vertical coordinate is sine theta. The point is therefore written as the ordered pair cosine theta, sine theta. In the second quadrant, x is negative while y is positive, so cosine is negative and sine is positive. In the third quadrant both are negative. In the fourth, cosine is positive and sine is negative. The exact values from the first quadrant survive; only their signs change. The right triangle has not vanished. Its horizontal and vertical projections have become directed coordinates following the point through every quadrant. Every point on this circle satisfies x squared plus y squared equals one. Substitute x equals cosine theta and y equals sine theta, and Pythagoras becomes cosine squared theta plus sine squared theta equals one. The theorem we met on the coordinate grid has become the most important trigonometric identity. Now keep the point rotating, place a graph beside the circle, and record its vertical coordinate as the angle steadily grows.

## Scene 08 — The Circle Draws Sine and Cosine — 17:30–20:15 — Voice Clip 08

As theta increases from zero, the point moves around the unit circle. On the graph, horizontal position records theta, while vertical position records sine theta, the point’s height. A guide line carries that height directly across, so the circle and graph remain visibly connected. Before each quarter-turn, predict the height. At ninety degrees, or pi over two radians, the point is at the top, so sine equals one. At one hundred and eighty degrees, or pi radians, it is on the middle line, so sine equals zero. At two hundred and seventy degrees, or three pi over two, it is at the bottom, so sine equals negative one. At three hundred and sixty degrees, or two pi, it returns to its starting height, so sine is zero again. Between those landmarks the motion stays smooth. The point climbs fastest near the middle, slows near the top, reverses direction, accelerates downward, and repeats below the axis. Recording every intermediate height joins the predictions into one continuous sine curve. Cosine comes from the same point, but records the horizontal coordinate. It begins at one when sine begins at zero. The two graphs have the same shape, but cosine is one quarter-turn ahead: cosine theta equals sine of theta plus pi over two. Continue beyond one revolution and the coordinates repeat. Each complete turn draws another identical cycle, so the period is two pi. A sine wave is circular height recorded against angle, or against time when the circle rotates steadily. This connection turns the letters in a wave equation into visible controls.

## Scene 09 — The Four Wave Controls — 20:15–22:10 — Voice Clip 09

Start with y equals A times sine of B theta plus C, then plus D. Each letter controls one visible feature. A controls amplitude, the distance from the middle line to a peak. In the circle picture, changing A is like changing the radius: a larger radius produces a taller wave and a smaller radius produces a shorter one. B controls how quickly the angle advances. Increase its magnitude and more cycles fit into the same horizontal distance, as if the wheel were turning more quickly. The period becomes two pi divided by the absolute value of B. C controls phase, telling us where the cycle begins. It is like placing the cabin at a different starting angle, so the wave shifts horizontally. D moves the middle line up or down. In the circle picture it changes the axle height; the rotation remains the same, but every recorded height moves with it. Radius, rotation speed, starting angle, and axle height: amplitude, frequency control, phase, and vertical shift. The equation is a compact set of motion instructions. We have learned how an angle produces an output. Now ask the reverse question: if we know the output, which angle produced it?

## Scene 10 — Inverse Trigonometric Functions — 22:10–25:40 — Voice Clip 10

Forward trigonometry begins with an angle and returns a ratio. Sine of thirty degrees is one half. Inverse trigonometry begins with that ratio and asks for an angle. Arcsine of one half is thirty degrees. The notation sine to the power negative one often means the inverse function, not one divided by sine. The reciprocal of sine is cosecant; inverse sine answers a different question. But there is a subtle problem. Draw a horizontal line at height one half across several cycles of the sine graph. It meets the curve again and again. Thirty degrees, one hundred and fifty degrees, and those angles plus complete turns all have the same sine. A function must return one output for one input, so arcsine chooses a principal branch where sine never doubles back: from negative ninety to positive ninety degrees. Within that restricted interval, every height from negative one to one corresponds to exactly one angle. Arccosine performs the same reversal for the horizontal coordinate and conventionally returns an angle from zero to one hundred and eighty degrees. Arctangent reverses a slope and returns an angle between negative ninety and positive ninety degrees, without including the endpoints. Think of the three questions visually. Arcsine asks, what angle gives this vertical height? Arccosine asks, what angle gives this horizontal position? Arctangent asks, what angle gives this rise-over-run slope? For example, arcsine of zero point five is thirty degrees. Arccosine of zero point five is sixty degrees. Arctangent of one is forty-five degrees. A calculator may show only those principal answers, even though a full trigonometric equation can have infinitely many solutions. To find all of them, return to the unit circle, use symmetry, and add complete turns. Inverse functions are therefore not a new universe. They run our existing projection backward, with a carefully chosen branch so the answer is unambiguous. This forward-and-backward relationship prepares us to manipulate trigonometric expressions without losing their geometric meaning.

## Scene 11 — The Identity Toolkit — 25:40–30:40 — Voice Clip 11

Trigonometric identities can look like a wall of unrelated formulas. But can one picture generate them? We need only three moves: resize a triangle, combine two turns, and reuse or reverse a turn. Start with the right triangle inside a unit circle. Its sides are cosine theta, sine theta, and one. Pythagoras gives cosine squared theta plus sine squared theta equals one. Now divide every length by cosine theta. The shape stays the same; only its scale changes. The base becomes one, the vertical side becomes tangent theta, and the hypotenuse becomes secant theta. So Pythagoras now reads one plus tangent squared theta equals secant squared theta. Return to the original triangle and divide by sine theta. Its sides become cotangent theta, one, and cosecant theta, giving cotangent squared theta plus one equals cosecant squared theta. One triangle has produced the entire Pythagorean family. The small equations below simply show how those new side names come from sine and cosine. Now combine two turns. Rotate through alpha, then beta. The gold radius ends at alpha plus beta. Split it into a green component along the first ray and a purple component sideways to it. Reading the vertical pieces gives sine of alpha plus beta equals sine alpha cosine beta plus cosine alpha sine beta. Reading the horizontal pieces gives cosine alpha cosine beta minus sine alpha sine beta. The minus sign appears because the purple horizontal piece points left. What if the second turn runs backward? Watch beta reverse. The signs change with it, producing the subtraction formulas. Now reuse a turn. Set beta equal to alpha. Two equal turns make two alpha, so sine two alpha equals two sine alpha cosine alpha. Cosine two alpha becomes cosine squared alpha minus sine squared alpha. Use the first Pythagorean identity and the same result can be written as one minus two sine squared alpha, or two cosine squared alpha minus one. They are interchangeable views, not extra facts. Finally, reverse the double-angle step. Replace the smaller angle by alpha over two and rearrange. We obtain the two half-angle identities shown on screen. If we later take a square root, the quadrant decides the positive or negative sign. So do not hunt through a formula wall. Ask for the move you need: resize, combine, reuse, or reverse. Choose the move and rebuild the identity. Next, we will carry that habit into triangles without a right angle.

## Scene 12 — Triangles That Are Not Right — 30:40–34:40 — Voice Clip 12

Consider a triangle with no right angle. Label the vertices A, B, and C, and label each opposite side with the corresponding lowercase letter. SOH–CAH–TOA cannot be applied directly because there is no hypotenuse. But we can create right triangles by dropping an altitude from one vertex to the opposite side. One oblique triangle splits into two familiar right triangles sharing the same height. In the left triangle, the height equals b times sine A. In the right triangle, the same height equals a times sine B. Because both expressions describe one physical length, b sine A equals a sine B. Rearranging gives a over sine A equals b over sine B. Repeating the argument from another vertex includes the third pair, producing the law of sines: a over sine A equals b over sine B equals c over sine C. It is especially useful when we know a side and its opposite angle. A longer side must face a larger angle, and the law preserves that pairing. There can be an ambiguous case when two different triangles share the supplied side, another side, and a non-included angle; the diagram, not blind substitution, tells us whether zero, one, or two triangles are possible. The law of cosines answers a different kind of problem. Look at one side as a horizontal projection plus a remainder. Apply Pythagoras to one of the smaller right triangles, substitute the projected length b cosine A, and simplify. The result is a squared equals b squared plus c squared minus two b c cosine A. If A equals ninety degrees, cosine A is zero and the final term disappears, leaving the ordinary Pythagorean theorem. So the law of cosines is Pythagoras with an angle correction. Use it when three sides are known, or when two sides and their included angle are known. The altitude also gives an area formula. Base times height over two becomes one half a b sine C when the height is expressed through sine. The choice of tool can now be visual. For a right triangle, use the basic ratios. For an opposite side-angle pair, consider the law of sines. For three sides or side-angle-side information, consider the law of cosines. Every non-right triangle hides right triangles inside it, so the original ratios never stopped working. We are now ready to leave drawn triangles and watch the same mathematics move physical air.

## Scene 13 — Trigonometry Becomes Sound — 34:40–36:30 — Voice Clip 13

When a sine signal reaches a speaker, the changing electrical value drives the cone forward and backward in a smooth repeating motion. The cone compresses and expands the surrounding air, creating a pressure wave that our ears detect as a pure tone. If the vibration repeats more times each second, we hear a higher pitch. If the pressure variation becomes larger, we usually hear a louder sound. Real musical sounds are more complicated than a single perfect sine. Imagine a fundamental wave, a smaller wave at twice its frequency, and another at three times its frequency. Add their values point by point and the result is no longer a simple sine curve, yet every component contributes to the combined shape. Changing the relative strengths of those components changes the tone’s character, or timbre. Similar combinations help describe mechanical vibration and alternating current. We should not claim that every visible wave is literally made by one rotating circle. The precise idea is more useful: projecting uniform circular motion creates a perfect sine wave, and sine waves can serve as building blocks for complex repeating signals. To understand exactly which building blocks are present, we change from the time view to the frequency view. That change of viewpoint is the central idea of Fourier analysis.

## Scene 14 — Fourier and the Motions Inside Sound — 36:30–41:00 — Voice Clip 14

Look at one complicated waveform. In the time view we can see how the signal changes from instant to instant, but its simple ingredients are hidden. Fourier analysis asks a different question: how much of each frequency is present? Begin with a slow sine wave. Add a smaller wave that completes two cycles in the same time. Add another at three times the first frequency, and another at five times. Each component has its own amplitude, frequency, and phase. When their vertical values are added point by point, the complicated waveform returns. Nothing was lost; we merely separated one shape into simpler motions. Now replace the stacked curves with a frequency spectrum. Horizontal position no longer represents time. It represents frequency. The height of each bar records the amplitude of that component. A tall bar at the fundamental means that frequency contributes strongly. Shorter bars at higher harmonics show smaller contributions. Phase information is also needed to reconstruct a general signal, although our simple bar picture focuses first on amplitude. The time view answers when the signal changes. The frequency view answers what oscillations it contains. A Fourier transform is the mathematical bridge between those views. The idea becomes especially vivid with a square wave. Start with one sine component. Its rounded shape is far from square. Add the third harmonic with one third of the amplitude. The top becomes flatter and the transition steeper. Add the fifth, seventh, and more odd harmonics, each smaller than the previous one. The curve approaches the square-wave target more closely. Near each sharp jump, small ripples remain; this overshoot is known as the Gibbs phenomenon and reminds us that infinitely sharp corners demand infinitely many frequencies. The demonstration does not mean every signal is periodic forever. Nonperiodic signals can also be analyzed using a continuous range of frequencies and appropriate versions of the Fourier transform. The central intuition remains: complicated change can be examined as a collection of simple rotating phases. Sound engineers use this to display spectra and shape timbre. Communication systems use it to separate frequency bands. Medical imaging, optics, vibration analysis, compression, and many differential equations use the same change of viewpoint. Every sine and cosine component can itself be represented by a rotating arrow. Add those arrows head to tail and their endpoint traces the reconstructed signal. Fourier analysis has therefore brought us back to circles, but now many circles rotate together. There is one final language that writes each rotating arrow in a remarkably compact way, joining exponential growth, imaginary numbers, sine, and cosine in a single formula.

## Scene 15 — Euler’s Formula — 41:00–44:00 — Voice Clip 15

Extend the ordinary number line into a plane. The horizontal axis contains real numbers. The vertical axis contains imaginary multiples of i, the number whose square is negative one. A point on this complex plane can be written as a real part plus i times an imaginary part. Now draw a unit circle and rotate a vector through angle theta. Its horizontal coordinate is cosine theta. Its vertical coordinate is sine theta, so the complex number at its tip is cosine theta plus i sine theta. Euler’s formula says that this same rotating point can be written as e to the power i theta. The exponential has not become an unrelated trick. With an imaginary exponent, it represents rotation instead of ordinary real growth. Multiplying by e to the i theta rotates a complex number through theta. Multiplying by another such factor adds another rotation, which is why exponential multiplication naturally reproduces the trigonometric addition formulas. Let the vector complete a full turn and e to the i two pi returns to one. Let theta equal pi, a half-turn. The point lands at negative one, so e to the i pi equals negative one. Move every term to one side and we obtain e to the i pi plus one equals zero, an equation connecting five central constants in one line. Its beauty comes from the geometry underneath it: a half-turn on the complex unit circle. Euler’s formula also explains why Fourier analysis can use complex exponentials instead of separate sines and cosines. A complex exponential is a rotating arrow; its real shadow is cosine and its imaginary shadow is sine. Right triangles, unit-circle coordinates, sine waves, Fourier components, and complex exponentials are not competing explanations. They are different languages for the same rotating motion. We can now assemble the entire course and see how each chapter grew from that one idea.

## Scene 16 — The Complete Synthesis — 44:00–45:00 — Voice Clip 16

An angle measured a turn. Coordinates gave the moving point an address, and Pythagoras connected its horizontal and vertical distances. Freezing the turn revealed a right triangle whose unchanging ratios became the six trigonometric functions. Two special triangles supplied exact values, and the unit circle extended those values through every quadrant. Recording the circle’s coordinates produced graphs; inverse functions ran those graphs backward. Identities described reciprocal views, distances, and combined rotations. Dropping an altitude carried the same ideas into every triangle. Physical vibration turned the graphs into sound, Fourier analysis separated complex signals into simple rotations, and Euler’s formula wrote each rotation as one complex exponential. Trigonometry is not a bag of disconnected formulas. It is the language of rotation seen through triangles, coordinates, and waves. Many formulas, one moving idea.
