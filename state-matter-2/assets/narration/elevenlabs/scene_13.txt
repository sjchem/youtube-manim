[warm, conversational delivery] Can we repair the ideal gas equation? In eighteen seventy-three, Johannes van der Waals did it with two corrections, one for each thing the model ignores. [short pause] First, attraction. Compare the gas with the same model after switching attraction off, while keeping its size, amount, volume and temperature unchanged. That reference pressure equals the measured pressure plus an attraction correction. For now, we isolate one effect; the size correction comes next. How big is the correction? Attraction acts between pairs of molecules. Count the nearby pairs in this box. [short pause] [curious] Now double the density. Twice as many molecules, and each has about twice as many neighbours. The number of interacting pairs grows about four times. So the correction grows with the square of the molar density, n over V. Van der Waals wrote it as a n squared over V squared. The constant a measures how strongly the molecules attract. For helium, it is tiny. For carbon dioxide, it is about a hundred times larger. For ammonia, larger still. [pause] This correction is an average over the whole gas, a mean-field estimate, not an exact count of every tug. But it captures how attraction scales.
