[warm, conversational delivery] Drop a steel ball into water, and into honey. [short pause] In water, it plunges. In honey, it crawls. At room temperature, honey can be thousands of times more viscous than water. Molecular structure and interactions decide viscosity. Honey is a concentrated solution of small sugar molecules, not a tangle of polymer chains. Hydrogen bonds, crowding and molecular rearrangement all matter. In other liquids, large tangled polymer molecules can also hinder flow. Attraction alone can't predict it. [pause] Warm a liquid, and its viscosity usually falls. Molecules have more energy to break free from their neighbours and rearrange. Water at one hundred degrees is less than a third as viscous as at twenty. That's why warm honey pours so much more easily. [pause] [curious] One famous myth deserves correcting. Old window panes are sometimes said to be thicker at the bottom because glass is a slow-flowing liquid. [short pause] [emphasized] It isn't. Ordinary glass is an amorphous solid: its atoms are disordered like a liquid's, but locked in place. At room temperature, it doesn't measurably flow, even over centuries. The uneven panes came from the way old glass was made.
