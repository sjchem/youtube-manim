[warm, conversational delivery] A whole distribution is hard to quote, so we summarise it with typical speeds. Here is nitrogen, at three hundred kelvin. [short pause] The peak is the most probable speed: more molecules move near this speed than near any other. It equals the square root of two R T over M. For nitrogen, about four hundred and twenty metres per second. The average speed adds up every speed and divides by the number of molecules. It is the square root of eight R T over pi M: about four hundred and eighty metres per second. The root mean square speed squares each speed, averages the squares, then takes the square root. It equals the square root of three R T over M: about five hundred and twenty metres per second. [pause] [curious] Why this order? The long tail of fast molecules pulls the average to the right of the peak. Squaring gives those fast molecules even more weight, so the root mean square speed lies further still. Most probable, average, root mean square: in the ratio one, to one point one two eight, to one point two two five. [pause] One detail. In these formulas, M is the molar mass in kilograms per mole. Use grams, and every speed comes out wrong, by a factor of about thirty-two.
