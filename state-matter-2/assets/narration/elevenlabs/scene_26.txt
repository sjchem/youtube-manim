[warm, conversational delivery] So a liquid minimises its surface. For a given volume, which shape has the least area? A sphere. [short pause] Reshape a cube of liquid into a sphere of the same volume, and its surface shrinks by about a fifth. Free of other forces, a drop pulls itself round. On a table, gravity and contact with the surface flatten it. Floating in orbit, a water drop is almost a perfect sphere. Surfaces also meet solids. Water wets clean glass: it is attracted to the glass, and climbs a narrow tube. The surface curves up at the edges. [short pause] Mercury does the opposite. Its atoms attract one another far more than they attract glass. Its surface curves down, and it sinks in the tube. Heat an ordinary liquid, and its surface tension usually falls. Thermal motion reduces the free-energy cost of the interface; it does not switch off the molecular attractions. [pause] [curious] Follow water all the way up. Its surface tension keeps dropping, and reaches zero at its critical temperature, about three hundred and seventy-four degrees Celsius. [short pause] [emphasized] Of course: at the critical point, the surface between liquid and vapour disappears.
