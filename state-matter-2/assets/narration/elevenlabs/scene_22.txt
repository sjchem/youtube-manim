[warm, conversational delivery] Now we can see why liquefaction needs the right combination of cooling and compression. Compression pushes molecules close together. Cooling slows them down, so that their attraction can hold them there. [short pause] Every gas has a critical temperature. Above it, squeezing alone never makes a separate liquid. Carbon dioxide's critical temperature is about thirty-one degrees Celsius, just above room temperature. That is why squeezing carbon dioxide at room temperature can liquefy it, as at the start of this lesson. [pause] Nitrogen's is about one hundred and twenty-six kelvin, nearly minus one hundred and fifty degrees Celsius. No amount of squeezing at room temperature will liquefy nitrogen. It must be cooled first. Ammonia's critical temperature is higher still: about four hundred and five point five kelvin. Its molecules attract strongly, partly through hydrogen bonds. [pause] [emphasized] For otherwise comparable molecules, stronger attraction tends to raise the critical temperature. Molecular size matters too. In the van der Waals model, the relevant balance is a divided by b, not a alone. A deeper attraction relative to the excluded volume resists more thermal motion.
