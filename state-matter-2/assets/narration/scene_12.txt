So every real gas holds a tug of war. Attraction pulls the pressure down. Size pushes it up. Which side wins depends on how hard we squeeze. At low pressure, the molecules are far apart. Neither effect matters much, and the gas is nearly ideal. At moderate pressure, the molecules are close enough to attract one another, but still have room to move. Attraction usually wins: a negative deviation. At very high pressure, the molecules are jammed together. Their size and their short-range repulsion take over: a positive deviation. Temperature matters too. Fast, hot molecules shrug off attraction more easily, so heating weakens the negative deviation. And the gas itself matters. Helium and hydrogen attract so weakly that at room temperature, size wins almost from the start. They show positive deviation across the pressures shown here. Quick check. A gas, at fixed amount, volume and temperature, shows a pressure below the ideal prediction. Which effect is winning? Attraction.
