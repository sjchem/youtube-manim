Temperature reshapes these curves. Here is Z for a van der Waals gas, as a surface over pressure and temperature. Near the critical temperature, Z dips deep below one. Slow molecules give attraction time to act. Warm the gas, and the dip gets shallower. At one special temperature, the curve leaves Z equals one perfectly flat. Attraction and size cancel at low pressure, and the gas behaves almost ideally over a low-pressure range. This is the Boyle temperature. The curve starts flat; it need not stay flat as pressure rises. Above it, size wins from the start, and Z rises. Every gas has its own. Measured, it is about three hundred and twenty-seven kelvin for nitrogen, but only about twenty-three for helium. So at room temperature, helium sits far above its Boyle temperature, and its Z rises from the start. One warning. Z equals one does not always mean the molecules ignore each other. On this curve, attraction and size are both strong. They nearly cancel here. An exact cancellation can also give Z equals one at a particular state; it does not make the whole gas ideal.
