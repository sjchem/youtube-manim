Here is something remarkable. Draw the van der Waals equation's own isotherms. At high temperature, they look like gas isotherms: squeeze, and the pressure rises smoothly. But below a certain temperature, each isotherm develops a wiggle. Along part of that wiggle, squeezing the fluid would lower its pressure. No stable fluid behaves that way. The equation is telling us that the single fluid splits into two phases. Replace the wiggle with a flat line, drawn so that the two areas it cuts off are equal. This is Maxwell's construction, and the flat line is the coexistence pressure, just as Andrews measured. The wiggles shrink as the temperature rises, and vanish at one point: the van der Waals critical point. Its temperature is eight a over twenty-seven R b. So an equation built only from attraction and size predicts condensation and a critical point. In practice, chemists often run this backwards, and choose a and b to match a gas's measured critical point.
