# Narration Script — Permutations & Combinations Explained Visually

Planned runtime: **26:45**. Each scene is one continuous spoken passage, ready
for recording or TTS. Only the scene headings carry timestamps; do not read
headings aloud. Transitions are part of the narration, including the handoff
into the next scene. Export with `python -m utils.narration_export`.

Read warmly and conversationally, with short breaths at punctuation. Continuous
means connected speech, not rushing or eliminating thinking time: leave the
three-second quiz countdowns silent. Chapter ranges are animation budgets,
not measured speech timings. Use the recording cues in `animation_plan.md` to
place natural pauses; check an actual recording against the animation before
final rendering. No voice recording is supplied with this script.

## Scene 01 — The lock's little secret (00:00 – 01:32)

Here is a combination lock. Three little wheels, and one code that opens it. Let's try one, two, three. Turn each wheel into place, pull the shackle, and it opens. Now close it and try something that seems almost the same. Keep every digit, but reverse their order: three, two, one. The lock stays shut. Nothing was added. Nothing was taken away. Yet the result changed. That is the little secret hiding in the name. In mathematics, a combination cares about which objects you choose. This lock also cares about where each digit goes. Its code is ordered. We will come back to codes that repeat digits; first, let's see the difference using three distinct letters. Replace one, two, three with A, B, C. In numbered positions, A B C and B A C are different arrangements. That is the permutation idea. Now put those same letters inside a circle and call them a team, with no special roles. Swap A and B again. The team still has exactly the same members. That is the combination idea. One swap, but two different meanings. How can we count both kinds of possibilities without writing out every answer? Our first clue comes from something much more ordinary: choosing what to wear.

## Scene 02 — Follow the branches (01:32 – 04:29)

Open a wardrobe. There are three shirts, red, blue and green, and two pairs of trousers. Every shirt can go with either pair. How many complete outfits can we make? Start with just the red shirt. Follow one branch to the light trousers and another to the dark trousers. Those are two different outfits. The blue shirt opens another two branches. So does the green shirt. Each path ends with one shirt and one pair of trousers, and every possible outfit has exactly one path. Count the ends: six. We could write six little descriptions, but the tree has already organised them for us. Three groups of two. Three times two equals six. Now let the clothes disappear and keep only the choices. The first stage has three options. From every one of those options, the second stage has two. That word, every, matters. We multiply because each branch offers the same number of next choices. The actual options can change later; the number at that stage must stay the same. If some branches have fewer options, we count those branches separately. Here, everything fits the simple pattern. Add two pairs of shoes, either of which works with every outfit. Each of our six paths splits in two, making twelve complete outfits. Three times two times two. This is the Fundamental Principle of Multiplication: when one complete outcome requires step one and step two, multiply their choices. There is a companion rule. Suppose you can reach school by one of three bus routes, or one of two train routes. A journey uses a bus route or a train route, never both. The two groups of possibilities do not overlap, so three plus two gives five journeys. Alternatives add; successive stages multiply. In counting language, OR usually points to addition, while AND usually points to multiplication. The important condition is that the alternatives do not describe the same outcome twice. We now have the two basic engines of counting. Next, what happens when making a choice removes an option? Let's put four books on a shelf and watch.

## Scene 03 — Every choice changes the pool (04:29 – 06:49)

Here are four different books and four empty positions. We want to use every book exactly once. The question is how many different shelves we can make. Begin at the first empty position. All four books are still available, so we have four choices. Pick one and move it onto the shelf. Watch the pool above it: that book has left. At the second position, only three books remain. Which three depends on our first choice, but there are always three. That is why multiplication still works. Choose another book and move it down. Now the third position has two possibilities. Either remaining book could go there, and each choice produces a different order on the shelf. Once we make that choice, the final position has only one possibility. One book, one space. There is no decision left, but there is still one way to complete the shelf. Multiply the choices we just watched: four times three times two times one. Twenty-four different arrangements. The shelf on screen is one example; the product counts all of them. This descending product appears so often that it has its own shorthand: four factorial. The exclamation mark means multiply down to one. For any positive whole number n, the same picture gives n factorial. We have simply named the shrinking-choice pattern. And that pattern grows astonishingly fast. Watch the totals climb; the bar uses a compressed logarithmic scale so they fit on screen. Eight books already give more than forty thousand arrangements. Twelve give nearly half a billion. Now suppose we only care about a few positions, rather than every position. Do we still need the whole factorial?

## Scene 04 — Stop at the podium (06:49 – 09:29)

Let's answer that with a race. Five different runners, three medals, and no ties. We are counting possible podium results. Gold, silver and bronze are separate positions: winning gold is a different outcome from winning bronze. Start with the highest step. Any of the five runners could take gold, so the first factor is five. Choose a winner and watch that runner leave the pool. There are four runners still available for the other medals. We are choosing possible results, one position at a time. Now fill silver. Whoever won gold cannot also take silver in this race, so there are four choices. The names left in the pool depend on the gold winner, but the number is always four. After silver is filled, three runners remain available for bronze. Choose one of them, and our podium is complete. Five times four times three: sixty possible podiums. The two remaining runners do not need to be ranked, because our question only asked about medals. That is the difference from the bookshelf. Five factorial would keep going, multiplying by two and then one to arrange fourth and fifth place as well. For every podium, that would count the same medal result twice, once for each order of the two remaining runners. Divide five factorial by two factorial, and those extra distinctions disappear. Why two? Five runners minus three medal positions leaves two runners unranked. We write this count as five P three: choose and arrange three of five distinct objects. Replace five with n, and three with r, and the same reasoning gives n factorial divided by the factorial of n minus r. Here n is the number available, and r is the number of positions we fill, with no object used twice. For our runners, one hundred and twenty divided by two gives sixty. But before we turn medals into teams, let's look closely at what makes two podiums different.

## Scene 05 — Same people, different story (09:29 – 10:57)

Take one possible result: A wins gold, B wins silver, and C wins bronze. Read the positions from left to right. Now swap A and B. Nobody new has joined the podium, and nobody has left it. We still have the same three people. Has anything changed? Think about it from A's point of view. A had gold and now has silver. B has made the opposite journey. The membership stayed the same, but the awards changed. That is why A B C and B A C are different outcomes. The slots carry information. We can see all the possibilities by moving these same three people through the three medal positions. Each new arrangement tells a different story about the race. There are three choices for gold, two for silver and one for bronze: six arrangements, or three factorial. A permutation records two things together: who was chosen and where each person ended up. So far every arrangement has been allowed. Real problems often add a rule: perhaps two objects must stay together, or two people must stay apart. Before removing order to form a team, let's learn to draw those restrictions so they become countable.

## Scene 06 — Make the restriction visible (10:57 – 13:57)

Arrange the four letters in MATH, but keep A and T together. Instead of testing every arrangement, bind A and T inside one visible block. Now we are moving three objects: M, the block, and H. Those three objects can be arranged in three factorial ways. But the block has an internal choice. It can read A T or T A, giving two factorial arrangements inside. Multiply the outside arrangements by the inside arrangements: three factorial times two factorial equals twelve. That is the block method. When objects must stay together, temporarily treat them as one object, arrange the larger objects, then arrange what is inside the block. The restriction has changed the objects we count, but the multiplication principle is still doing the arithmetic. Now reverse the kind of restriction. Arrange three distinct boys and two distinct girls in a row so that the girls are never adjacent. First arrange the three boys. That gives three factorial orders. Once they are placed, look at the spaces they create: one before the row, two between neighbouring boys, and one after the row. Four gaps. To keep the girls apart, choose two different gaps from those four. That gives four choose two. The girls are distinct, so they can exchange their chosen gaps in two factorial ways. Multiply the stages: three factorial times four choose two times two factorial. The answer is seventy-two. Notice why the gap method works. Placing at most one girl in each gap makes adjacency impossible automatically. We did not subtract bad arrangements after creating them; we built only valid arrangements. If the people were not distinct, or if several could share a gap, the factors would change, so always translate the exact rule before calculating. Together suggests a block. Apart suggests gaps. These are not extra formulas to memorise. They are ways to redraw a restriction until the multiplication principle can see it. Now return to our five people. Remove the medal positions and every restriction, and ask only which three belong to a science team.

## Scene 07 — Six stories become one team (13:57 – 17:12)

Here are our five people again, now choosing a science team of three. The letters keep track of who is who, but nobody will be first, second or third on the finished team. Let's try the podium method anyway and see exactly what it counts. Five choices for the first name, four for the second, and three for the third. That makes sixty ordered lists. It is a perfectly good count of lists. The question is whether every list gives a different team. Focus on A, B and C. Our method produces A B C, A C B, B A C, B C A, C A B and C B A. Six separate cards. On a podium, every card meant something different. On this team, none of those positions has a role. Watch the cards move into the same circle. Whichever card we start from, the people inside are A, B and C. All six lists describe one team. So the count is too large, and now we can see exactly why. Three distinct people can be rearranged in three factorial ways, which is six. The important part is that this happens for every team, not just A, B and C. Pick any other three people: there are still three choices for the first name, two for the second and one for the last. Every team has exactly six list versions. That equal repetition is what lets us divide. Sixty ordered lists, divided into groups of six, gives ten teams. Here they are. Each green circle has a different membership, and no circle needs a first, second or third position. Now the notation has something concrete to stand for. Five choose three means the number of these teams. We found it by taking five P three and dividing by three factorial. Substitute the permutation formula, and we get five factorial divided by three factorial times two factorial. For n distinct objects with r chosen, the same reasoning gives n factorial divided by r factorial times the factorial of n minus r. The two divisors have different jobs: one removes arrangements of the objects left out; the other removes arrangements inside the chosen group. A combination is a permutation with the unnecessary order divided away. You don't have to remember that as a trick. Remember the six cards entering one circle. Whenever each real outcome has the same number of counted versions, division removes the repeats. Let's see if you can recognise which kind of outcome a question is asking for.

## Scene 08 — Put the idea to work (17:12 – 18:42)

Three quick situations. In each one, ask: if I swap the selected objects, does the outcome change? First, a four-digit PIN. Compare one, five, two, eight with eight, two, five, one. The digits are the same, but the code is different, so order matters. Can a digit repeat? If every position accepts zero through nine, there are ten choices each time: ten to the fourth, or ten thousand. The pool does not shrink, so the no-repeat permutation formula does not apply. Next, choose mushroom, olive and cheese as pizza toppings. Move them around. We still chose the same topping types. Listing order creates no new selection, so this is a combination. Finally, give Alice and Bob two different roles. Alice is president and Bob is vice-president. Swap them. Their jobs change, so this is a permutation. Now remove the roles and place the same people inside one team. Swap them again. The team still contains Alice and Bob, so this is a combination. The people did not decide the method; the meaning of the outcome did. Ask whether a swap changes the result. If yes, order matters. If no, count the group once. Then ask whether objects may repeat. Next comes another kind of repetition: separate objects that look alike.

## Scene 09 — Invisible swaps and geometric choices (18:42 – 21:42)

Consider the letters in LEVEL. There are five positions, so treating every letter as distinct would give five factorial, or one hundred and twenty arrangements. But swap the first L with the last L. The written word does not change. Those two Ls are alike, so every visible arrangement was counted two factorial times. The same problem occurs with the two Es. Swapping them also produces no new visible arrangement. Divide both invisible swaps away: five factorial over two factorial times two factorial equals thirty distinct arrangements. In general, when n objects include p alike objects of one kind, q of another kind, and so on, divide n factorial by p factorial, q factorial, and the remaining repetition factorials. The repetition counts must add up to n. This is the same deeper idea we used for combinations: if several counted arrangements look identical under the rules of the problem, keep only one by dividing by the equal overcount. The objects are physically separate tiles in our picture, but their labels make certain swaps indistinguishable. Now move from words to geometry. Place six labelled vertices around a hexagon. Every pair of vertices determines one line segment, so there are six choose two, or fifteen segments. A pair has no first vertex and second vertex, which is exactly why this is a combination rather than a permutation. But six of those segments are already the sides of the hexagon. Dim those boundary edges and the nine remaining segments are diagonals. Therefore the number of diagonals in an n-sided polygon is n choose two minus n. The choice itself creates the geometry: choose two vertices and you get a segment. Choose three non-collinear points and you get a triangle, so n suitable points determine n choose three triangles. The non-collinear condition matters; three points on one straight line do not enclose a triangle. This is why combinations appear in geometry. We are not selecting a committee, but the mathematical action is identical: choose an unordered set of vertices. Next, return to our ten students. Choosing who travels also determines exactly who stays behind.

## Scene 10 — One choice, two descriptions (21:42 – 23:10)

Imagine ten students and three places on a trip. Choose three students and light them up. Ten choose three gives one hundred and twenty possible groups. Now look at the seven unlit students. Did we make a separate decision about them? No. The moment we chose the three travellers, we also determined exactly which seven would stay behind. Every group of three has one matching group of seven. And every group of seven left behind points back to exactly one group of three travelling. Nothing is missing, and nothing is counted twice. Flip the highlight to see the other description of the same decision. That is why ten choose three equals ten choose seven: one hundred and twenty either way. For n objects, choosing r determines the remaining n minus r. The two counts must match. We can see the same symmetry across the whole row. Choosing none pairs with choosing all. Choosing one pairs with leaving one out. For these ten students, the largest count sits in the middle, at five. The picture explains the identity before we do any algebra. Now let's use that same idea of an unordered group somewhere the number of possibilities becomes much harder to picture: a deck of cards.

## Scene 11 — Count what you cannot list (23:10 – 24:32)

Take a standard deck of fifty-two distinct cards, with no jokers. Deal five cards without putting any back. Before reaching for a formula, decide what we are counting: the five cards in the hand, regardless of their dealing order. Move them around. Fan them differently. The same five cards are still there. Changing the display has not created a new hand. So we need fifty-two choose five. In the formula, fifty-two factorial contains a long tail beginning at forty-seven factorial. Cancel that shared factor, and only five factors remain on top: fifty-two, fifty-one, fifty, forty-nine and forty-eight. Divide by five factorial because each five-card set has one hundred and twenty possible orders. The result is two million, five hundred and ninety-eight thousand, nine hundred and sixty. Almost two point six million hands. The drifting pictures suggest the scale; they are only a tiny illustration of that total. We have counted the possibilities without listing them. There is just one small boundary case left to understand: what should our formulas say when nothing is left to arrange?

## Scene 12 — One empty arrangement (24:32 – 25:18)

Choose all five students from a group of five. There is exactly one possible team: everybody. Our combination formula should agree. Put five into both places, and the denominator contains five factorial times zero factorial. After the five factorials cancel, the answer is one divided by zero factorial. For this formula to include choosing everybody, we define zero factorial as one. And there is a picture behind that definition. An empty box has one arrangement: the empty arrangement. No objects does not mean no possible outcome. It means the one outcome contains nothing. This also keeps the factorial pattern consistent at its boundary. Now we can bring the whole story together.

## Scene 13 — Return to the lock (25:18 – 26:30)

The whole journey grew from the same picture: objects moving into positions. Arrange all n distinct objects, and the shrinking choices give n factorial. Fill only r positions, and stop the product early: n P r. Keep only the chosen group, and divide by its r factorial orderings: n choose r. The pictures tell us what each formula counts. Restrictions did not replace these ideas: blocks, gaps, alike objects and geometric selections simply redrew the outcome so the same principles could count it. First ask whether order is part of the outcome. Then check the rules: these factorial formulas use distinct objects without repetition. A code that allows repeated digits needs a different choice count, as we saw. Now return to our lock. Reverse its digits, and you have a different code. Make the same rearrangement inside a team, and the membership stays unchanged. The motion is similar; the meaning is different. That is the question to take with you: when I rearrange the same objects, have I created something new? Once you know what makes an outcome different, you can build the count from the picture.

## Scene 14 — Keep seeing the mathematics (26:30 – 26:45)

Thank you for watching. If these pictures helped the mathematics make sense, subscribe for more visual journeys. Stay curious, keep asking what changes, and keep looking for the idea behind the formula.
