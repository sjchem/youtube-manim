Consider the letters in LEVEL. There are five positions, so treating every letter as distinct would give five factorial, or one hundred and twenty arrangements. But swap the first L with the last L. The written word does not change. Those two Ls are alike, so every visible arrangement was counted two factorial times. The same problem occurs with the two Es. Swapping them also produces no new visible arrangement. Divide both invisible swaps away: five factorial over two factorial times two factorial equals thirty distinct arrangements. In general, when n objects include p alike objects of one kind, q of another kind, and so on, divide n factorial by p factorial, q factorial, and the remaining repetition factorials. The repetition counts must add up to n. This is the same deeper idea we used for combinations: if several counted arrangements look identical under the rules of the problem, keep only one by dividing by the equal overcount. The objects are physically separate tiles in our picture, but their labels make certain swaps indistinguishable. Now move from words to geometry. Place six labelled vertices around a hexagon. Every pair of vertices determines one line segment, so there are six choose two, or fifteen segments. A pair has no first vertex and second vertex, which is exactly why this is a combination rather than a permutation. But six of those segments are already the sides of the hexagon. Dim those boundary edges and the nine remaining segments are diagonals. Therefore the number of diagonals in an n-sided polygon is n choose two minus n. The choice itself creates the geometry: choose two vertices and you get a segment. Choose three non-collinear points and you get a triangle, so n suitable points determine n choose three triangles. The non-collinear condition matters; three points on one straight line do not enclose a triangle. This is why combinations appear in geometry. We are not selecting a committee, but the mathematical action is identical: choose an unordered set of vertices. Next, return to our ten students.
