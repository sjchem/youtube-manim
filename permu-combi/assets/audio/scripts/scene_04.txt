Let's answer that with a race. Five different runners, three medals, and no ties. We are counting possible podium results. Gold, silver and bronze are separate positions: winning gold is a different outcome from winning bronze. Start with the highest step. Any of the five runners could take gold, so the first factor is five. Choose a winner and watch that runner leave the pool. There are four runners still available for the other medals. We are choosing possible results, one position at a time. Now fill silver. Whoever won gold cannot also take silver in this race, so there are four choices. The names left in the pool depend on the gold winner, but the number is always four. After silver is filled, three runners remain available for bronze. Choose one of them, and our podium is complete. Five times four times three: sixty possible podiums. The two remaining runners do not need to be ranked, because our question only asked about medals. That is the difference from the bookshelf. Five factorial would keep going, multiplying by two and then one to arrange fourth and fifth place as well. For every podium, that would count the same medal result twice, once for each order of the two remaining runners. Divide five factorial by two factorial, and those extra distinctions disappear. Why two? Five runners minus three medal positions leaves two runners unranked. We write this count as five P three: choose and arrange three of five distinct objects. Replace five with n, and three with r, and the same reasoning gives n factorial divided by the factorial of n minus r. Here n is the number available, and r is the number of positions we fill, with no object used twice. For our runners, one hundred and twenty divided by two gives sixty.
