Imagine ten students and three places on a trip. Choose three students and light them up. Ten choose three gives one hundred and twenty possible groups. Now look at the seven unlit students. Did we make a separate decision about them? No. The moment we chose the three travellers, we also determined exactly which seven would stay behind. Every group of three has one matching group of seven. And every group of seven left behind points back to exactly one group of three travelling. Nothing is missing, and nothing is counted twice. Flip the highlight to see the other description of the same decision. That is why ten choose three equals ten choose seven: one hundred and twenty either way. For n objects, choosing r determines the remaining n minus r. The two counts must match. We can see the same symmetry across the whole row. Choosing none pairs with choosing all. Choosing one pairs with leaving one out. For these ten students, the largest count sits in the middle, at five. The picture explains the identity before we do any algebra.
