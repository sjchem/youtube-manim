## Scene 03 — The pull of the nucleus — 02:17–03:22

Start with the simplest picture. The nucleus is positive. Electrons are negative. Opposite charges attract, so the nucleus pulls on every electron around it. Two things set the strength of that pull. The first is charge. Add protons, and the nucleus pulls harder. Twice the charge, twice the pull, if nothing else changes. The second is distance. Move the electron farther away, and the pull fades quickly. Double the distance, and the attraction drops to a quarter. So the pull depends on charge and distance, like this. One important word before we go on. These clouds are not little planets on orbits. The dots sample where electrons may be found. They are not separate particles; denser regions indicate higher probability. The cloud has no hard edge. Our contour is a visual guide; actual atomic radii come from measurements of bonded atoms.

## Scene 04 — Shielding and the charge an electron feels — 03:22–04:47

But an outer electron is never alone with the nucleus. Look at sodium. Eleven protons, and ten inner electrons, packed closer to the nucleus than the outer one. Those inner electrons repel the outer electron. They partly cancel the nucleus's pull. We call this shielding. So the outer electron does not feel all eleven protons. It feels a smaller, effective charge. We call it effective nuclear charge, Z effective: the positive charge an electron feels after shielding. Now watch the two effects compete. First, add protons while the new electrons join the same shell. Electrons in the same shell shield each other poorly. So each new proton wins. Z effective rises, and the cloud is pulled inward. Now add a new shell. The new outer electron starts farther out, outside a larger core of inner electrons. The extra protons are largely cancelled by the extra inner electrons, and distance takes over. The cloud expands. Charge pulls the cloud in. Distance and shielding let it spread out. Every trend in this video is a contest between them. Now we can solve our first mystery.

## Scene 05 — Across a period, atoms shrink — 04:47–06:18

Experiment one: walk across period two, from lithium to fluorine. Here is lithium, and here is its size: one hundred and fifty-two picometres. A picometre is a trillionth of a metre. Beryllium has one more proton, and its new electron joins the same second shell. The pull grows faster than the shielding. The cloud shrinks to one hundred and eleven. Boron. Carbon. Nitrogen. Oxygen. Fluorine. Each step adds a proton to the nucleus and an electron to the same shell. And each time, the cloud is drawn in a little closer. By fluorine, the radius is sixty-four picometres, less than half of lithium's. Plot the numbers, and the graph falls all the way across. One honest note: these sizes come from bonded atoms. Metals are measured in metallic crystals, non-metals in covalent bonds. The two methods give somewhat different numbers, but on either scale, the trend is the same. Across a period, atoms generally shrink. Repeat the walk in period three, from sodium to chlorine. One hundred and eighty-six picometres shrinks to ninety-nine. Same pattern, one shell further out. Across a period, the growing nuclear charge generally wins. But what happens when we go down?

## Scene 06 — Down a group, atoms grow — 06:18–07:38

Experiment two: start at lithium and walk down group one. Sodium has eight more protons, but also a whole new shell. Its outer electron sits in three s, outside a neon-like core. Potassium adds another shell. Then rubidium. Then caesium. Each new shell sits farther out, and every inner shell shields the outer electron from the extra protons. So the atoms grow: one fifty-two, one eighty-six, two thirty-one, two forty-four, two sixty-two picometres. Caesium is about one and three quarter times the size of lithium. The halogens tell the same story, from fluorine at sixty-four to iodine at one hundred and thirty-three. Now an important warning. These spheres are a picture, not reality. An atom has no hard surface. Its cloud thins out gradually. A radius is a useful measure taken from atoms in bonds or crystals, not the location of a wall. Across a period, nuclear pull wins. Down a group, new shells and shielding win. But what if an atom loses an electron?

## Scene 07 — Losing and gaining electrons — 07:38–08:52

Take a sodium atom. Eleven protons, eleven electrons. Its lone three s electron is loosely held. Give it enough energy, and it leaves. Sodium becomes a sodium ion, with a positive charge. Does it just become an atom with one less dot? No. The ion is dramatically smaller. One hundred and eighty-six picometres for the atom. Ninety-five for the ion. Why? The whole outer shell is gone. What remains is the compact, neon-like core, pulled by eleven protons with only ten electrons to hold. Now go the other way. Fluorine accepts an extra electron. The fluoride ion has nine protons, but ten electrons. Electron repulsion grows, and the same nuclear charge is shared among more electrons. So the cloud swells. Sixty-four picometres for the atom, one hundred and thirty-six for the ion. Atomic and ionic sizes are measured in different ways, so compare them as a pattern, not to the last picometre. Losing electrons shrinks. Gaining electrons swells.

## Scene 08 — Same electrons, different size — 08:52–09:43

Here is a stranger comparison. Oxide, fluoride, sodium and magnesium ions. Count their electrons. Ten, ten, ten and ten. Exactly the same arrangement. Only the nucleus differs: eight, nine, eleven and twelve protons. Same electrons, more protons, stronger pull. Watch the clouds contract. One hundred and forty, one hundred and thirty-six, ninety-five, sixty-five picometres. The magnesium ion is less than half the size of the oxide ion, with the very same electrons. Even with the same number of electrons, the nucleus can completely change the size. Size tells us how close the electrons stay. Next question: how hard is it to pull one away?

## Scene 09 — How hard is it to steal an electron? — 09:43–10:57

If an electron sits close to a strong nucleus, it should be harder to remove. Let us test that. Chemists measure this with the first ionization enthalpy: the energy to remove the outermost electron from an isolated atom in the gas phase. X in the gas phase becomes X plus, and an electron. Put lithium on the left and neon on the right. Same shell, very different grip. Now pour in energy. Slowly raise the meter. At about five hundred and twenty kilojoules per mole, lithium's outer electron escapes. Neon's electron is still there. Keep going. One thousand. Fifteen hundred. Two thousand. Only at about two thousand and eighty does neon let go. Four times the energy. Lithium's electron is far out and feels a weak effective charge. Neon's is closer, and feels a much stronger one. Smaller atom, tighter grip, higher ionization enthalpy. One trend explains another. So what happens if we measure it for every element?

## Scene 10 — The ionization mountain range — 10:57–12:05

Here is the first ionization enthalpy for the first eighty-six elements, in order of atomic number. It rises and falls in a repeating pattern. The peaks are the noble gases: helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon. Closed shells hold on tightly. Right after every peak comes a cliff. Lithium, sodium, potassium, rubidium, caesium. One electron in a fresh shell, easy to remove. Turn the graph into a landscape, and the periodic pattern becomes a mountain range. Each climb is one period: ionization enthalpy generally rises across a row. And the peaks get lower as we go down. Larger atoms, more shielding, an easier escape. So ionization enthalpy generally increases across a period and decreases down a group: the mirror image of atomic size. But look closely at period two. The climb is not smooth. Two steps go the wrong way.

## Scene 11 — When the trend seems to break — 12:05–13:30

Beryllium: nine hundred kilojoules per mole. Boron: eight hundred and one. The trend says boron should be higher. The answer is in the orbitals. Beryllium's outer electrons fill two s. Boron's extra electron goes into two p, a higher-energy subshell. A two p electron is shielded a little more by the two s pair, and starts higher up in energy. So less energy is needed to remove it. The general trend is not broken. We simply removed a different kind of electron. The second surprise: nitrogen, fourteen hundred and two. Oxygen, thirteen hundred and fourteen. Nitrogen has three p electrons, one in each p orbital, all unpaired. Oxygen's fourth p electron must share an orbital with another electron. Two electrons in the same orbital repel each other strongly. That repulsion makes one of them easier to remove. So oxygen's first ionization enthalpy is a little lower than nitrogen's. Period three repeats both dips: magnesium to aluminium, and phosphorus to sulfur. The periodic trend isn't broken. We simply need a more detailed picture of the orbitals. Removing an electron is one experiment. Next, we reverse it.

## Scene 12 — Adding an electron instead — 13:30–14:32

Instead of pulling an electron away, send one in. Electron gain enthalpy is the energy change when a gas-phase atom accepts an electron. X in the gas phase, plus an electron, becomes X minus. If energy is released, the value is negative. The more negative, the more strongly the atom welcomes the electron. Chlorine releases a lot of energy. It is one electron short of a closed outer set, and the newcomer completes it. Neon's shells are closed. The newcomer would need a higher shell. A free neon atom does not hold a stable negative ion. Across a period, electron gain enthalpy generally becomes more negative, towards the halogens. Down a group, it generally becomes less negative, because the added electron lands farther from the nucleus. Generally. Because this trend has a famous exception, right at the top.

## Scene 13 — Fluorine versus chlorine — 14:32–15:38

Fluorine or chlorine: which releases more energy when it gains an electron? Fluorine is smaller. Its nucleus is closer. Surely fluorine wins. Fluorine: minus three hundred and twenty-eight kilojoules per mole. Chlorine: minus three hundred and forty-nine. Chlorine releases more. Fluorine's five two p electrons occupy a compact region. The next electron must enter that same crowded region. A new electron must squeeze into that small space, and it is pushed back by strong electron-electron repulsion. Chlorine's three p region is larger. The newcomer has more room, so the repulsion is weaker. Both atoms attract the electron strongly. In fluorine, more of that attraction is cancelled by crowding. Size helps, but crowding matters too. That is why chlorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy of all the elements. But there is another way an atom can attract electrons, without completely gaining or losing one.

## Scene 14 — A tug of war inside a bond — 15:38–16:40

Inside a molecule, atoms share electrons. Here is hydrogen chloride. Draw the shared pair as an even cloud between the two atoms. That is a convenient first sketch, but not what happens. Chlorine pulls the shared electrons towards itself. The density shifts. Chlorine gains a small negative charge, hydrogen a small positive one. This tendency has a name: electronegativity. An atom's pull on the electrons it shares in a bond. Keep three ideas separate. Ionization enthalpy removes an electron from an isolated atom. Electron gain enthalpy adds one to an isolated atom. Electronegativity describes a tug of war for shared electrons, inside a bond. It is a relative scale, not a single measured energy. On Pauling's scale, hydrogen is about two point two, chlorine about three point two. The bigger the difference, the more lopsided the sharing.

## Scene 15 — Electronegativity across the table — 16:40–17:44

Height now represents electron density. This landscape illustrates unequal sharing; it is not a calculated molecular orbital. Two identical atoms share evenly: a symmetric ridge. Now make one partner more electronegative. The ridge leans. Increase the difference, and the shared density slides further towards the stronger pull. Large differences suggest more ionic character. There is no universal cutoff between ionic and covalent bonding. Where are the strongest pullers? Light up the table by electronegativity. It generally increases across a period and up a group, towards the upper right. Fluorine sits at the top, with a Pauling value of about four. Caesium, at the lower left, is among the lowest. Same reason as before: a small atom with a high effective nuclear charge pulls bonding electrons closer. Now we know which atoms hold electrons tightly, and which pull on shared ones. Can we predict chemistry?

## Scene 16 — Sodium gives, chlorine takes — 17:44–19:08

Sodium and chlorine. One gives, one takes. Sodium has one outer electron, far from the nucleus, with a low ionization enthalpy. Chlorine is one electron short of a closed outer set, with a strongly negative electron gain enthalpy. In a simplified model, sodium's electron moves to chlorine. Sodium shrinks into a small positive ion. Chlorine swells into a larger negative ion. Now the two ions attract each other strongly. Careful, though. Removing sodium's electron costs more energy than chlorine releases by taking it. On its own, the transfer is uphill. What makes salt stable is what comes next: many ions attract one another and pack into a crystal. Each sodium ion is surrounded by six chloride ions, and each chloride by six sodium ions. Building the lattice releases energy; atomization costs must also enter the complete balance. Ionization and electron gain tell us which way electrons tend to move. The whole energy story decides whether a compound forms. Swap sodium for potassium, or chlorine for bromine, and a similar story plays out. Because the trends live in the table, the table can predict the chemistry.

## Scene 17 — A map of chemical behaviour — 19:08–20:22

Let the table become a map of tendencies, rather than positions. On the left, the alkali and alkaline earth metals. Low ionization enthalpies. They tend to lose electrons and form positive ions. Metallic character generally grows down a group and towards the left. In the upper right, the non-metals. Oxygen, and the halogens most of all, attract electrons strongly. On the far right, the noble gases. Closed shells, very high ionization enthalpies, and generally little chemistry. Between them, metallic character fades into non-metallic character across each period. The same trend shows up in oxides. Sodium oxide dissolves to give a basic solution. Move across the period, and the oxides turn acidic. Sulfur and chlorine form oxides that make acids in water. Basic on the left, acidic on the right. We will save the full oxide story for another episode. One idea links all of it: how tightly each atom holds its electrons, and how strongly it attracts more. Now let us see every trend at once.
