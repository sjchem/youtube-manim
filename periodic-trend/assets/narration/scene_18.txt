Here is the full table, back in its familiar form. Press the first tab: atomic radius. Height and colour show size, for the main-group elements, all on one covalent scale. Tall, warm towers in the lower left. Small atoms in the upper right. Radius generally increases down and to the left. Switch to ionization enthalpy. Watch the landscape flip. Now the towers rise in the upper right, peaking at helium. Ionization enthalpy generally increases up and to the right. Switch to electronegativity. The upper right rises again, with fluorine on top. Last tab: electron gain enthalpy, how much energy is released when an electron is added. The halogens tower over everything, with chlorine highest. Nitrogen, beryllium, magnesium and the noble gases need energy instead. These trends have real exceptions. Four maps. Four broad patterns, clearest for the main-group elements. Not laws, but tendencies with reasons. Size, down and left. Ionization enthalpy and electronegativity, up and right. Electron gain, more negative toward the upper right, apart from its exceptions. Now, back to our opening mystery. Across period two, each step adds a proton and an electron to the same shell. Shielding is weak, the pull grows, the cloud shrinks. Down group one, each step adds a new shell. Inner electrons shield the extra protons, distance grows, and the cloud expands. Adding particles shrinks the atom when they tighten the pull, and grows it when they open a new shell. And the same competition explains ionization, electron gain, electronegativity and reactivity. What looked like a collection of unrelated arrows now has a physical explanation. How far are the electrons? How strongly does the nucleus attract them? And how are the electrons arranged? Three questions, and the periodic table becomes a map of invisible forces.
