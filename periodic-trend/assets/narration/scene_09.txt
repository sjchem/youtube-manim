If an electron sits close to a strong nucleus, it should be harder to remove. Let us test that. Chemists measure this with the first ionization enthalpy: the energy to remove the outermost electron from an isolated atom in the gas phase. X in the gas phase becomes X plus, and an electron. Put lithium on the left and neon on the right. Same shell, very different grip. Now pour in energy. Slowly raise the meter. At about five hundred and twenty kilojoules per mole, lithium's outer electron escapes. Neon's electron is still there. Keep going. One thousand. Fifteen hundred. Two thousand. Only at about two thousand and eighty does neon let go. Four times the energy. Lithium's electron is far out and feels a weak effective charge. Neon's is closer, and feels a much stronger one. Smaller atom, tighter grip, higher ionization enthalpy. One trend explains another. So what happens if we measure it for every element?
