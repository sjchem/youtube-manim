Experiment two: start at lithium and walk down group one. Sodium has eight more protons, but also a whole new shell. Its outer electron sits in three s, outside a neon-like core. Potassium adds another shell. Then rubidium. Then caesium. Each new shell sits farther out, and every inner shell shields the outer electron from the extra protons. So the atoms grow: one fifty-two, one eighty-six, two thirty-one, two forty-four, two sixty-two picometres. Caesium is about one and three quarter times the size of lithium. The halogens tell the same story, from fluorine at sixty-four to iodine at one hundred and thirty-three. Now an important warning. These spheres are a picture, not reality. An atom has no hard surface. Its cloud thins out gradually. A radius is a useful measure taken from atoms in bonds or crystals, not the location of a wall. Across a period, nuclear pull wins. Down a group, new shells and shielding win. But what if an atom loses an electron?
