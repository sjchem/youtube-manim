Blow into a balloon, and it swells. Pump up a bicycle tyre, and it turns hard enough to carry your weight. Yet the air inside looks like nothing at all. No colour. Almost no weight. Just empty space. But that empty space is pushing. Near sea level, the pressure force on one square metre equals the weight of about ten tonnes. Opposite sides usually balance; a pressure difference gives the net push. So what is doing the pushing? Imagine shrinking yourself millions of times, and stepping inside. You would not find a smooth, continuous substance. You would find a crowd: countless molecules, each one moving in a straight line until it strikes something, and then flying off in a new direction. Many of them strike the walls. And every strike is a tiny push. One push is far too small to feel. But there are so many molecules, striking so often, that together they can hold up a car. None of these molecules knows Boyle's law, or the ideal gas equation. Yet together, they obey those laws with remarkable precision. How can random motion produce such predictable behaviour? This is The Molecular Dance.
