Boyle's question: what happens when you squeeze a gas? In sixteen sixty-two, Robert Boyle trapped some air in the sealed end of a J shaped glass tube, and poured mercury into the open end. The more mercury he added, the harder it pressed, and the smaller the trapped air became. Let's look inside. Trap a fixed amount of gas under a piston, and keep its temperature constant. The gauge reads one bar. Push the piston down slowly, letting heat escape, so the temperature never changes. The molecules move exactly as fast as before. Their average kinetic energy hasn't changed. But now they have less room. Each one crosses the box sooner, and returns to the walls more often. Watch the piston. The hits come thicker and faster. Halve the volume, and the hits on each square centimetre double. So does the pressure. Pressure rose, and no molecule got faster. Plot pressure against volume, and you trace a curve: a hyperbola. P is proportional to one over V. And here's the hidden simplicity. At every point on the curve, the rectangle P times V has the same area. P V is constant. P one V one equals P two V two.
