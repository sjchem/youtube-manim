Try this one. A gas occupies two litres at three hundred kelvin. It is heated at constant pressure to four hundred and fifty kelvin. What is the new volume? Temperature rose by half, from three hundred to four hundred and fifty kelvin. So the volume rises by half: three litres. Now a trap. Warm a gas from twenty to thirty degrees Celsius. The Celsius number rises by fifty percent, but absolute temperature does not. But the volume grows by only about three percent. In kelvin, twenty degrees Celsius is two hundred and ninety-three point one five kelvin, and thirty degrees is three hundred and three point one five. A rise of only a few percent. Gas laws need absolute temperature. In SI we use kelvin: the scale on which classical ideal-gas translational energy extrapolates to zero at zero kelvin.
