[warm, conversational delivery] One molecule gives a sputtering force: long gaps, and sudden spikes. Five molecules give a busier pattern, but it's still ragged. Fifty, and the relative fluctuations shrink. Five thousand, and the force is nearly smooth. Each trace is divided by its own mean so we can compare fluctuations, not absolute forces. [short pause] [emphasized] In real air, about three times ten to the twenty-three molecules strike every square centimetre of a wall, every second. An ordinary pressure gauge averages these tiny impacts into a steady reading. So add up all N molecules. The total force on the wall is N m times the average of v x squared, over L. Pressure is force per unit area. Divide by the wall's area, L squared, and L cubed is the volume, V. P equals N m times the average of v x squared, over V. [pause] [emphasized] Pressure is not something stored inside a gas. It is the steady flow of momentum, from molecules to the walls.
