[warm, conversational delivery] Take a helium atom. On average, its electron cloud is perfectly symmetrical around the nucleus. [short pause] But the electrons are always moving. At any instant, the cloud may be a little lopsided, with slightly more charge on one side. That makes a temporary dipole: one end slightly negative, the other slightly positive. [short pause] [curious] Now bring a second atom nearby. The temporary dipole pushes the neighbour's electrons away from its negative end. The neighbour becomes a dipole too, an induced dipole, lined up to attract. The flicker keeps changing direction, but the neighbour follows it, so the attraction never averages away. This is the London dispersion force. It is short ranged. The interaction energy falls roughly as one over r to the sixth. Double the distance, and the magnitude of that energy drops about sixty-four times. And it grows with the size of the electron cloud. Bigger clouds are easier to distort: they are more polarizable. [pause] Look at the noble gases. Helium boils at about four kelvin. Xenon, with its far larger cloud, boils at about one hundred and sixty-five. Dispersion acts between every pair of atoms and molecules, polar or not.
