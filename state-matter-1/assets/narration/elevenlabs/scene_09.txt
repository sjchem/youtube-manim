[warm, conversational delivery] So two effects compete. Attractions pull molecules together. Thermal motion shakes them apart. Watch sixty-one atoms that attract each other, in a real, two dimensional simulation. When the thermal energy is small compared with the depth of the well, they lock into a crystal. Each atom only vibrates about its place. A solid. [short pause] Warm them, and the atoms begin to slip past one another, while still holding together. A liquid. [short pause] Now give them far more energy than the well can hold. The atoms fly apart and fill the whole container. A gas. The state of matter depends on this balance between intermolecular attraction and thermal energy, together with the pressure. Argon's well is only about one kilojoule per mole deep, less than half of R T at room temperature. That's why argon is a gas in this room, and turns liquid only near eighty-seven kelvin. In a dilute gas, molecules are far apart and spend almost all their time flying freely. So as a first approximation, we ignore their attractions entirely and treat them as tiny points.
