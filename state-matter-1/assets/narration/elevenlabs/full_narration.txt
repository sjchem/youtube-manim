[warm, conversational delivery] Blow into a balloon, and it swells. Pump up a bicycle tyre, and it turns hard enough to carry your weight. [short pause] Yet the air inside looks like nothing at all. No colour. Almost no weight. Just empty space. [short pause] [emphasized] But that empty space is pushing. And every strike is a tiny push. One push is far too small to feel. But there are so many molecules, striking so often, that together they can hold up a car. None of these molecules knows Boyle's law, or the ideal gas equation. Yet together, they obey those laws with remarkable precision. [short pause] [curious] How can random motion produce such predictable behaviour? [pause] [warmly] This is The Molecular Dance.

[warm, conversational delivery] Here is the puzzle at the heart of this lesson. Up close, everything looks random. Follow one molecule: repeated collisions change its direction. Tracking every molecule in a real gas is impractical. [short pause] Step back, and the randomness disappears. The pressure gauge holds steady. So does the thermometer. Pressure, volume and temperature can be measured, and predicted. The same thing happens with a crowd of people. You can't predict where one person in a busy station will walk next. But you can predict how many will pass through the gates each minute. [short pause] [emphasized] The gas laws describe the crowd, not the individual. To understand them, we will follow one chain of reasoning, in four questions. What holds molecules together, and what lets them separate? Why does a gas push on its container? Why do pressure, volume and temperature follow simple rules? And can one equation explain them all? [pause] [warmly] Let's begin with the forces between molecules.

[warm, conversational delivery] Start with a single water molecule. Its oxygen and two hydrogens are held together by covalent bonds. These are forces inside the molecule: intramolecular forces. But a glass of water holds trillions of trillions of molecules, and they cling to one another. Forces between molecules are called intermolecular forces. [short pause] [emphasized] For water, these intermolecular links are much weaker. Breaking an O H bond inside water takes about four hundred and sixty kilojoules per mole. A typical attraction between neighbouring water molecules is only about twenty. Boiling water doesn't break water molecules apart. It only pulls whole molecules away from their neighbours. [pause] [curious] So where does an attraction between neutral molecules come from? There are several mechanisms. And the simplest one works even for atoms with no polarity at all.

[warm, conversational delivery] Take a helium atom. On average, its electron cloud is perfectly symmetrical around the nucleus. [short pause] But the electrons are always moving. At any instant, the cloud may be a little lopsided, with slightly more charge on one side. That makes a temporary dipole: one end slightly negative, the other slightly positive. [short pause] [curious] Now bring a second atom nearby. The temporary dipole pushes the neighbour's electrons away from its negative end. The neighbour becomes a dipole too, an induced dipole, lined up to attract. The flicker keeps changing direction, but the neighbour follows it, so the attraction never averages away. This is the London dispersion force. It is short ranged. The interaction energy falls roughly as one over r to the sixth. Double the distance, and the magnitude of that energy drops about sixty-four times. And it grows with the size of the electron cloud. Bigger clouds are easier to distort: they are more polarizable. [pause] Look at the noble gases. Helium boils at about four kelvin. Xenon, with its far larger cloud, boils at about one hundred and sixty-five. Dispersion acts between every pair of atoms and molecules, polar or not.

[warm, conversational delivery] Some molecules carry a dipole all the time. In hydrogen chloride, chlorine pulls the shared electrons toward itself. The chlorine end is slightly negative, and the hydrogen end slightly positive. A permanent dipole. [short pause] [curious] When two such molecules meet, orientation matters. Head to tail, opposite charges are closest, and they attract. Side by side and pointing opposite ways, they attract too, more weakly. [short pause] [emphasized] But head to head, like charges face each other, and they repel. For fixed orientations, this interaction energy falls off as one over r cubed, and its sign depends on the angles. Notice that this is an energy, not a force. In a gas, though, molecules tumble constantly. If every orientation were equally likely, attraction and repulsion would cancel exactly. [short pause] [emphasized] They are not equally likely. Attractive orientations have lower energy, so thermal motion visits them a little more often. The average comes out attractive. And this averaged attraction falls off as one over r to the sixth, the same distance law as dispersion.

[warm, conversational delivery] A permanent dipole can also act on a molecule that has none. Bring hydrogen chloride near an argon atom. The dipole's charges distort argon's electron cloud, and create an induced dipole, aligned to attract. This dipole induced dipole attraction also falls off as one over r to the sixth. [pause] [curious] Now a particularly important, directional attraction: the hydrogen bond. It carries a partial positive charge, not a full positive charge. That spot is drawn to a lone pair on a neighbouring molecule. In water, each molecule can join up to four of these links, building a shifting three dimensional network. Ammonia forms them too, through the lone pair on its nitrogen. [short pause] [emphasized] The effect is dramatic. Hydrogen sulfide, a heavier molecule with a similar shape, boils at about minus sixty degrees Celsius. Water, with its hydrogen bonds, boils at one hundred. Hydrogen bonds hold liquid water together, and explain many of its unusual properties.

[warm, conversational delivery] If molecules attract, why don't they simply collapse into one another? Push two atoms closer and closer. At first, the attraction grows. [short pause] [emphasized] But when their electron clouds begin to overlap, a fierce repulsion takes over. The electrons resist being squeezed into the same space, and the nuclei repel each other too. Put attraction and repulsion together, and you get the full interaction energy: a steep wall, a well, and a long, shallow tail. A common model is the Lennard-Jones curve. Its repulsive term rises as one over r to the twelfth. Its attractive term falls as one over r to the sixth. At the bottom of the well is the distance where the two are balanced. That is where neighbours in a liquid or a solid tend to settle. [pause] [curious] The depth of the well tells us how strongly molecules hold on to each other. So if they attract, why doesn't every gas collapse into a liquid?

[warm, conversational delivery] Because molecules never sit still. Gas particles move continuously. Molecules can also rotate and vibrate. Here, we focus on their translational motion through the container. Temperature is related to average energy per particle, rather than the total energy of the sample. For a classical ideal gas, average translational kinetic energy is proportional to absolute temperature. [short pause] [emphasized] Average K equals three halves k B T. Here k B is the Boltzmann constant, about one point three eight times ten to the minus twenty-three joules per kelvin, and T is measured in kelvin. [short pause] Notice the word average. At any instant, some molecules are slow and some are fast. Temperature describes the whole distribution, not any one molecule. Heat the gas, and the whole distribution shifts to higher speeds. At room temperature, the average is tiny: about six times ten to the minus twenty-one joules per molecule. But per mole, the thermal energy scale, R T, is about two and a half kilojoules.

[warm, conversational delivery] So two effects compete. Attractions pull molecules together. Thermal motion shakes them apart. Watch sixty-one atoms that attract each other, in a real, two dimensional simulation. When the thermal energy is small compared with the depth of the well, they lock into a crystal. Each atom only vibrates about its place. A solid. [short pause] Warm them, and the atoms begin to slip past one another, while still holding together. A liquid. [short pause] Now give them far more energy than the well can hold. The atoms fly apart and fill the whole container. A gas. The state of matter depends on this balance between intermolecular attraction and thermal energy, together with the pressure. Argon's well is only about one kilojoule per mole deep, less than half of R T at room temperature. That's why argon is a gas in this room, and turns liquid only near eighty-seven kelvin. In a dilute gas, molecules are far apart and spend almost all their time flying freely. So as a first approximation, we ignore their attractions entirely and treat them as tiny points. That's the ideal gas model. [pause] [curious] But even then, something remains. What happens when those moving molecules strike a wall?

[warm, conversational delivery] [emphasized] This is the turning point of our story. Where does gas pressure come from? Start with just one molecule, of mass m, bouncing around inside a cube of side L. Focus on one wall, and on one part of the velocity: the part perpendicular to that wall, v x. The molecule strikes the wall and bounces back elastically. Its speed is unchanged. Its motion along the wall is unchanged. Only v x flips sign. Its momentum along x goes from plus m v x to minus m v x. A change of two m v x. That momentum doesn't vanish. The wall receives it: a push of two m v x, outward, every time. Take a nitrogen molecule whose speed perpendicular to the wall is about five hundred metres per second. Its momentum transfer is around five times ten to the minus twenty-three kilogram metres per second. [short pause] [curious] Utterly tiny. So how can tiny pushes add up to the pressure of the air?

[warm, conversational delivery] Two things matter: how big each push is, and how often it comes. After hitting the wall, our molecule must cross the box and come back before it can strike again: a distance of two L, at speed v x. So the time between hits is two L over v x. On a graph of force against time, each hit is a sharp spike. Its area is the momentum delivered: two m v x. [short pause] Now spread each spike evenly over the time until the next one. The area stays the same, but the force becomes steady. Average force equals the push, divided by the time between pushes: two m v x, over two L over v x. The twos cancel, and one v x moves up. The average force is m v x squared over L. [short pause] [warmly] Faster molecules push harder, and they come back sooner. That's why the speed appears squared.

[warm, conversational delivery] One molecule gives a sputtering force: long gaps, and sudden spikes. Five molecules give a busier pattern, but it's still ragged. Fifty, and the relative fluctuations shrink. Five thousand, and the force is nearly smooth. Each trace is divided by its own mean so we can compare fluctuations, not absolute forces. [short pause] [emphasized] In real air, about three times ten to the twenty-three molecules strike every square centimetre of a wall, every second. An ordinary pressure gauge averages these tiny impacts into a steady reading. So add up all N molecules. The total force on the wall is N m times the average of v x squared, over L. Pressure is force per unit area. Divide by the wall's area, L squared, and L cubed is the volume, V. P equals N m times the average of v x squared, over V. [pause] [emphasized] Pressure is not something stored inside a gas. It is the steady flow of momentum, from molecules to the walls.

[warm, conversational delivery] But molecules don't only move along x. They fly in every direction. [short pause] Gather every velocity arrow at one point. Their tips form a fuzzy ball. No direction is special. Each velocity has three parts: v x, v y and v z. By Pythagoras in three dimensions, v squared equals v x squared plus v y squared plus v z squared. Average over the molecules. Small samples fluctuate, but at equilibrium, with no preferred direction, the three mean squares are equal. So the average of v x squared is exactly one third of the average of v squared. [short pause] [warmly] That one third is three dimensional space, showing up in the equation. Put it into our pressure. P V equals one third N m times the average of v squared. This is the pressure equation of the kinetic theory of gases.

[warm, conversational delivery] Now connect pressure to temperature. The average kinetic energy is one half m times the average of v squared. So one third N m times that average is two thirds N times the average kinetic energy. P V equals two thirds N times average K. And we already know the average kinetic energy: three halves k B T. [short pause] Substitute, and the two thirds and the three halves cancel. P V equals N k B T. Finally, count molecules in moles. N is n times Avogadro's number, and Avogadro's number times k B is the gas constant, R. [pause] [emphasized] P V equals n R T. The ideal gas law, built from nothing but moving molecules and bouncing walls. Notice the difference. Temperature tracks the average energy of each molecule. Pressure also depends on how many molecules there are, and how much room they have. [pause] [warmly] You won't be asked to derive this in Class eleven. But it shows why the gas laws work. Now let's watch each of them happen.

[warm, conversational delivery] Boyle's question: what happens when you squeeze a gas? In sixteen sixty-two, Robert Boyle trapped some air in the sealed end of a J shaped glass tube, and poured mercury into the open end. The more mercury he added, the harder it pressed, and the smaller the trapped air became. [short pause] Let's look inside. Trap a fixed amount of gas under a piston, and keep its temperature constant. The gauge reads one bar. Push the piston down slowly, letting heat escape, so the temperature never changes. The molecules move exactly as fast as before. Their average kinetic energy hasn't changed. But now they have less room. Each one crosses the box sooner, and returns to the walls more often. Watch the piston. The hits come thicker and faster. Halve the volume, and the hits on each square centimetre double. So does the pressure. [short pause] [emphasized] Pressure rose, and no molecule got faster. Plot pressure against volume, and you trace a curve: a hyperbola. P is proportional to one over V. [pause] [curious] And here's the hidden simplicity. At every point on the curve, the rectangle P times V has the same area. P V is constant. P one V one equals P two V two.

[warm, conversational delivery] Let's try it. A sample occupies four litres at one bar. It is compressed, at constant temperature, to two litres. [short pause] Pause and predict the new pressure. P two equals P one V one, over V two: one bar times four litres, over two litres. Two bar. [short pause] [emphasized] Half the volume, twice the pressure. Another way to see it: plot P against one over V, and the curve becomes a straight line through the origin. Each hyperbola belongs to one temperature. It's called an isotherm. Warm the gas, and the molecules hit harder at every volume, so the whole curve moves outward. Higher temperature, a larger constant: P V grows in step with T. We'll see exactly why in a moment.

[warm, conversational delivery] Boyle's law hides a second idea. Squeeze a gas into half the space, and the same molecules are packed twice as densely. At constant temperature, density is proportional to pressure. [short pause] You can feel this on a mountain. Air at sea level is squeezed by the weight of all the air above it. Every breath holds far fewer oxygen molecules. That's why climbers struggle, and why many carry oxygen. [pause] One more warning. Boyle's law needs constant temperature. [warm, conversational delivery] Charles' question: what happens when you heat a gas, but keep its pressure constant? Put a freely sliding piston on top, loaded with a weight. [warm, conversational delivery] Here's where Charles' law reveals something deep. Measure a gas's volume at several temperatures in Celsius, and plot the points. They fall on a straight line.
