Some molecules carry a dipole all the time. In hydrogen chloride, chlorine pulls the shared electrons toward itself. The chlorine end is slightly negative, and the hydrogen end slightly positive. A permanent dipole. When two such molecules meet, orientation matters. Head to tail, opposite charges are closest, and they attract. Side by side and pointing opposite ways, they attract too, more weakly. But head to head, like charges face each other, and they repel. For fixed orientations, this interaction energy falls off as one over r cubed, and its sign depends on the angles. Notice that this is an energy, not a force. In a gas, though, molecules tumble constantly. If every orientation were equally likely, attraction and repulsion would cancel exactly. They are not equally likely. Attractive orientations have lower energy, so thermal motion visits them a little more often. The average comes out attractive. And this averaged attraction falls off as one over r to the sixth, the same distance law as dispersion.
