Inside versus between. One 3D water molecule turns: its covalent O-H bonds glow (intramolecular). The camera pulls back to a tetrahedral cluster of five waters held by dashed green hydrogen bonds (intermolecular). ### Scene 04 · London dispersion forces · 03:49 (90.9s)

*Flickering clouds, induced dipoles, −1/r⁶, noble-gas boiling points* — `manim_scenes.scene_04_dispersion.Scene04Dispersion`

London dispersion. A helium atom's electron cloud, symmetric on average, flickers lopsided: a temporary dipole (delta labels follow the bulge). A second atom's cloud responds through the dipole field (``common.induced_offset``): head to tail along the axis, opposite when side by side, so the pair always attracts. The energy falls as -1/r^6 (double the distance: 1/64). Bigger clouds distort more; the noble gases' boiling points climb from He to Xe (CRC values, said with "about").

### Scene 05 · Permanent dipoles · 05:20 (81.4s)

*3D HCl poses; tumbling dipoles: Keesom average measured* — `manim_scenes.scene_05_dipoles.Scene05Dipoles`

Permanent dipoles. A 3D hydrogen chloride molecule: chlorine pulls the shared electrons (a soft cloud gathers on Cl), leaving delta-minus on Cl and delta-plus on H. Two molecules take three poses, and a live meter reads the dipole-dipole energy from ``physics_models.dipole_energy``: head to tail -2, side by side antiparallel -1, head to head +2 (units of mu^2 / 4 pi eps0 r^3). Then they tumble through a seeded Metropolis walk (``tumbling_dipoles``): the plain average of all orientations is zero, but the running mean of the thermally sampled energy settles below zero — the Keesom attraction, measured — and it falls as -1/r^6.

### Scene 06 · Induced dipoles and hydrogen bonds · 06:41 (83.3s)

### Scene 07 · Too close: repulsion · 08:04 (74.1s)

## Chapter — Where gas pressure comes from

### Scene 10 · One molecule, one wall · 12:13 (80.1s)

*One molecule in a 3D cube; elastic bounce: Δp = 2m vₓ* — `manim_scenes.scene_10_one_wall.Scene10OneWall`

One molecule, one wall. A single gold molecule zigzags inside a 3D cube of side L, its trail glowing; one wall is singled out. A 2D close-up of that wall shows the elastic bounce: the velocity splits into v_x (blue, toward the wall) and the part along the wall (rose); only v_x reverses. The momentum along x goes from +m v_x to -m v_x, so the wall receives 2 m v_x, outward, every hit. For N2 at 300 K (rms ~517 m/s) that is ~4.8e-23 kg m/s.

### Scene 11 · From hits to force · 13:33 (77.4s)

*Spikes every 2L/vₓ spread into F = m vₓ²/L* — `manim_scenes.scene_11_hits_to_force.Scene11HitsToForce`

From hits to force. One molecule bounces between two walls a distance L apart at speed v_x (``physics_models.single_molecule_hits``); a force-time graph below records a spike at each hit on the right wall, every 2L / v_x. Each spike's area is the impulse 2 m v_x. Spread each spike evenly over the interval to the next and the rectangles tile into a flat line of the same total area: the average force. The algebra follows on screen: 2 m v_x / (2L / v_x) = m v_x^2 / L.

### Scene 12 · A crowd of molecules · 14:51 (80.7s)

*1, 5, 50, 5000 molecules: force smooths; P = N m⟨vₓ²⟩/V* — `manim_scenes.scene_12_crowd.Scene12Crowd`

A crowd of molecules. The force on one wall, computed by ``physics_models.wall_force_trace`` for 1, 5, 50 and 5000 molecules with Maxwell-Boltzmann speeds: sputtering spikes become a ragged pattern, then an almost flat line (fluctuations shrink like one over the square root of the hits). In real air ~3e23 hits strike each square centimetre every second: a 3D crowd sparks on its right wall. Summing N molecules and dividing by the wall's area L^2 gives P = N m <v_x^2> / V.

### Scene 13 · Why one third? · 16:11 (72.3s)

*3D velocity ball; ⟨vₓ²⟩ = ⟨v²⟩/3; PV = ⅓ N m⟨v²⟩* — `manim_scenes.scene_13_one_third.Scene13OneThird`

Why one third? Molecules fly in every direction. Their velocity arrows, gathered at one point on a 3D stage, end in a fuzzy round ball: no direction is special. One velocity splits into blue, rose and purple components, v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2. Bars of the sample's averages <v_x^2>, <v_y^2>, <v_z^2> (computed from the film's gas) come out equal, so <v_x^2> = <v^2>/3, and the pressure equation becomes PV = 1/3 N m <v^2>.

### Scene 14 · From motion to temperature · 17:24 (83.6s)

*PV = ⅔ N⟨K⟩ → N k_B T → n R T* — `manim_scenes.scene_14_temperature.Scene14Temperature`

From motion to temperature. The kinetic pressure equation is rewritten step by step in one place: 1/3 N m <v^2> = 2/3 N (1/2 m <v^2>) = 2/3 N <K>; with <K> = 3/2 k_B T the fractions cancel to PV = N k_B T; and with N = n N_A and N_A k_B = R it becomes PV = nRT. A small live box keeps moving beside the algebra. Two boxes at the same temperature but different numbers of molecules then show the difference between temperature (energy per molecule) and pressure (also how many, in how much room).

Thinner air. The same molecules squeezed into half the room are packed twice as densely: at constant temperature, density is proportional to pressure. ## Chapter — Charles' law and absolute zero

### Scene 18 · Heat it, let it grow · 22:49 (84.9s)

*Weighted piston + heater: V ∝ T; hot-air balloon* — `manim_scenes.scene_18_charles.Scene18Charles`

Charles' law. A free piston loaded with a weight holds the pressure; a hot plate heats the gas. The molecules warm and speed up; the piston lags the heating a little, so the gauge first rises above 1 bar (the push beats the weight), then falls back as the volume grows and the hits thin out. V follows absolute temperature: 2 L at 300 K, 3 L at 450 K, 4 L at 600 K, all on a line through the origin. A hot-air balloon closes it: heated air expands, spills out, and the lighter air inside floats.

### Scene 19 · Absolute zero · 24:14 (76.8s)

*V–θ lines meet at −273.15 °C; Kelvin scale* — `manim_scenes.scene_19_absolute_zero.Scene19AbsoluteZero`

Absolute zero by extrapolation. Volume against Celsius temperature for three samples (different amounts or pressures): measured points between 0 and 150 C lie on straight lines with different slopes. Extended backward (dashed, purple), every line reaches zero volume at the same point, -273.15 C. Sliding the vertical axis there gives the Kelvin scale, T = theta + 273.15, and every line passes through the origin. A caution: real gases condense long before; the meeting point is an extrapolation.

### Scene 20 · Kelvin, not Celsius · 25:31 (70.8s)

*300 → 450 K: 3 L; the Celsius trap (≈3 %)* — `manim_scenes.scene_20_kelvin.Scene20Kelvin`

Kelvin, not Celsius. Worked example at constant pressure: 2 L at 300 K heated to 450 K. The viewer predicts; the weighted piston rises and the model gives 3 L (temperature up by half, volume up by half). Then the trap: 20 C to 30 C is a 50 % rise on the Celsius scale, but on the Kelvin scale (293.15 K to 303.15 K) it is only about 3 %, and so is the volume change. Paired bars make the difference visible.

## Chapter — Gay-Lussac's law

### Scene 21 · Heat it, hold it · 26:42 (80.3s)

*Rigid box heated: P ∝ T; 1.00 → 1.20 bar; sealed gas cylinder* — `manim_scenes.scene_21_gay_lussac.Scene21GayLussac`

Gay-Lussac's law. A rigid, bolted box on a hot plate: the volume and amount cannot change. Heating speeds the molecules: the gold molecule's velocity arrow lengthens (more momentum per hit) and the measured hits on the lid per second climb (more hits). The gauge, reading the model, goes from 1.00 bar at 300 K to 1.20 bar at 360 K, on a P-T line through the origin. Both effects scale with speed, so P scales with v^2, i.e. with T. A sealed gas cylinder closes the scene.

### Scene 22 · Same cause, different outcome · 28:02 (57.8s)

*Charles vs Gay-Lussac side by side; tyres* — `manim_scenes.scene_22_side_by_side.Scene22SideBySide`

Same cause, different outcome. Two identical samples heated from 300 K to 450 K side by side: a free, weighted piston (Charles) and a rigid lid (Gay-Lussac). The same microscopic change (molecules speed up) gives a larger volume at 1.00 bar on the left and 1.50 bar in the same volume on the right, both read from the model. A car tyre after a long drive is the rough everyday case (not perfectly rigid; no numbers claimed).

Avogadro's law. A weighted piston holds the pressure and the temperature is steady; a valve pumps molecules into the box (the gas model activates them at the inlet). ### Scene 24 · Equal volumes, equal numbers · 30:17 (89.9s)

Equal volumes, equal numbers. Three equal boxes at the same temperature and pressure hold helium, nitrogen and carbon dioxide. ## Chapter — The ideal gas equation

### Scene 25 · Four laws, one surface · 31:47 (90.1s)

*Four laws → PV = nRT; the 3D P–V–T surface and its slices* — `manim_scenes.scene_25_ideal_surface.Scene25IdealSurface`

Four laws, one surface. A table lists each law with the two quantities it holds constant; the volume relations combine into V ~ nT/P, then PV = nRT. Every state of one mole of ideal gas becomes one curved 3D surface, P = RT/V, computed from R in ``physics_models`` (V from 8 to 40 L, T from 150 to 600 K). Slices light up: constant T gives Boyle's hyperbola, constant V gives Gay-Lussac's straight line, constant P gives Charles' straight line. A bead rides each slice; the surface keeps turning.

### Scene 26 · Using P V equals n R T · 33:17 (87.2s)

*R = 8.314; one mole fills 25 L; weather balloon 3.33 L* — `manim_scenes.scene_26_using_ideal.Scene26UsingIdeal`

Using PV = nRT. R = 8.314 J/(mol K) is Avogadro's number times the Boltzmann constant (the same constant inside the kinetic theory); in litre-bar units R = 0.08314. One mole at 300 K and 1 bar fills 24.94 L, a cube about 29 cm on a side (3D box). For a fixed amount the combined law P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2 follows; a weather balloon (2 L, 1 bar, 300 K) rises to 0.5 bar and 250 K: halving P doubles V to 4 L, cooling takes a sixth off, 3.33 L. Every number comes from ``physics_models``.

### Scene 27 · Weighing a gas · 34:44 (65.7s)

*ρ = PM/RT: N₂ 1.12 g/L, He 0.16 g/L; M from ρ* — `manim_scenes.scene_27_density.Scene27Density`

Weighing a gas. Writing n = m / M turns PV = nRT into rho = PM / RT: at the same pressure and temperature, density follows molar mass. Two equal boxes of nitrogen and helium (same number of molecules, helium's moving faster) have densities of 1.12 and 0.160 g/L at 1 bar and 300 K, a ratio of 7; a helium balloon rises. Reversed, a measured density gives the molar mass: 1.12 g/L -> 28 g/mol, nitrogen. The model's two assumptions are named in purple: no size, no attractions.

## Chapter — Dalton's law of partial pressures

### Scene 28 · Mixtures of gases · 35:50 (103.0s)

*Two gases, separate hits; P = P_A + P_B; 2 + 1 bar; Everest O₂* — `manim_scenes.scene_28_dalton.Scene28Dalton`

Dalton's law. One box holds two ideal gases: 100 blue nitrogen and 50 rose oxygen molecules (2 mol : 1 mol, oxygen slightly slower). Each species strikes the walls independently; per-species hit counters measure it, and each partial pressure is what that gas alone would exert. Stacked bars add to the total: P_total = P_A + P_B = sum P_i, P_i = n_i RT / V = x_i P_total. Worked example: 3 bar total gives 2 bar and 1 bar. Back to the mountain: oxygen is 21 % of air, 0.21 bar at sea level, 0.07 bar on Everest.

### Scene 29 · Gas over water · 37:33 (62.7s)

*Gas over water: 1.000 − 0.0317 = 0.968 bar* — `manim_scenes.scene_29_over_water.Scene29OverWater`

Gas collected over water. Bubbles from a generating flask rise into an upturned jar in a water trough; the gas pushes the water level down. Water molecules (cyan) evaporate into the collected hydrogen (gold), adding their own partial pressure. Stacked bars: P_total = P_dry + P_water, so P_dry = P_total - P_water. At 298 K the aqueous tension is 0.0317 bar (NCERT Table 5.2): 1.000 bar total gives 0.968 bar of dry gas.
