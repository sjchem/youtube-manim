## Description

Oxygen's Lewis structure shows a double bond with every electron neatly paired
— yet liquid oxygen poured past a strong magnet is pulled into the gap and
hangs there. That only happens with unpaired electrons. Where are they hiding?

This video builds molecular orbital theory from waves. Atomic orbitals are
wave functions with a sign; adding them in phase piles electron density
between the nuclei (bonding), subtracting them leaves a node (antibonding), and
N independent basis orbitals give N molecular orbital combinations within a finite LCAO basis. We watch computed
σ and π orbitals form in 3D, see why antibonding rises more than bonding
falls (with the real LCAO energy curve of H₂⁺), and use bond order to explain
H₂⁺, H₂ and why the elementary model gives He₂ no net covalent bond. A weak dispersion-bound helium dimer lies beyond that model. Then we build the second-period MO
diagram, learn why boron-to-nitrogen and oxygen-to-fluorine use different
orderings (s–p mixing), fill nitrogen (triple bond, all paired) — and fill
oxygen, where Hund's rule puts the last two electrons in separate π\* orbitals.
Bond order two, two unpaired electrons: paramagnetic. Finally: why removing an
electron strengthens O₂'s bond, the superoxide and peroxide ions, B₂, F₂ and a
diagram challenge.

Part 3 of the Chemical Bonding series, following "Chemical Bonding, Explained
Visually" and "Molecular Geometry, Explained Visually".

Every number in the video is checked against data and the model:
bond lengths and dissociation energies (Huber & Herzberg / NIST), orbital
energies (Miessler, Fischer & Tarr), the helium dimer (Grisenti et al. 2000).
Energy-level spacings in the diagrams are schematic; the orderings are real.
