Back to the mystery we started with. Here is oxygen's Lewis structure. A double bond, and two lone pairs on each atom. Twelve valence electrons, every one of them in a pair. The magnet says otherwise. So let's take these electrons out of the Lewis picture, and put them where quantum mechanics says they belong. First, the right ordering. For oxygen, sigma two p sits below the pi pair. Now let them in. Sigma two s, two electrons. Sigma star two s, two more. The lower bonding and antibonding pair are full. Sigma two p, two. That is another occupied bonding pair. The pi pair takes four. That's ten so far, the same ten as nitrogen. Two electrons left. And the only place for them is the next level up: the pi star pair. Two degenerate orbitals. Two electrons. Remember Hund's rule. Spread out first. Each line is a different orbital with equal energy. One goes into the first pi star orbital. Watch the first gold arrow settle. And the other doesn't pair with it. Pairing up would cost energy. So it takes the second pi star orbital, spin parallel. Opposite spins are required only when sharing one orbital. These electrons occupy different orbitals. There they are. Two unpaired electrons, in two antibonding pi star orbitals. Invisible in the Lewis structure. Unavoidable in the molecular orbital diagram. Both count as antibonding electrons when we calculate bond order. Those parallel spins give oxygen a magnetic moment, rather like tiny magnets. In an applied field, their response helps draw liquid oxygen toward the stronger field. Oxygen is paramagnetic, and molecular orbital theory predicts it. The diagram explains the experiment's missing clue. Here are those two orbitals in three dimensions. Two pi star orbitals at right angles, each holding a single electron. The Lewis structure had no way to show them. The wave picture has no way to hide them. That is the oxygen mystery, solved.
