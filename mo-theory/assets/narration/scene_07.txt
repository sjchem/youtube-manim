Now we can build molecules, one electron at a time. Begin with the simplest molecule there is: two protons, and just one electron. H two plus. Its total charge is positive. Where does the electron go? Into the lowest orbital available, sigma one s. One electron occupies a state over both nuclei. To keep score, we'll use a quantity called bond order. Count the electrons in bonding orbitals, subtract the ones in antibonding orbitals, and divide by two. Only occupied orbitals enter this count. For H two plus: one bonding electron, none antibonding. One minus zero, over two. A bond order of one half. The antibonding orbital is still empty. Half a bond! A single electron can hold two nuclei together. It does not mean half a molecule. And this isn't just bookkeeping. H two plus really exists. It forms in electric discharges through hydrogen gas, and its bond length has been measured. Our simple wave combination already predicts an energy valley. The exact solution has a deeper one, but both agree: it's bound. So it doesn't always take a shared pair to make a bond. One well-placed electron can be enough.
