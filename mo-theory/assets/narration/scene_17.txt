Now the bond order. Bonding electrons: two, two, and four. Eight. Include the lower sigma pair too. Antibonding: two in sigma star two s, and two in pi star. Four. Eight minus four, over two. Two. This is the net covalent bond order. A double bond, just like the Lewis structure said. So the Lewis structure got the bond order right. What it missed was where two of the electrons live. Molecular orbital theory gives both answers at once: a double bond, and two unpaired electrons. Neither result requires adding an extra electron. And now the experiment makes sense. Pour liquid oxygen between the poles. Each molecule carries two parallel unpaired spins. The field favours alignment, pulling the liquid toward its strongest region. The drawing exaggerates that alignment. So the liquid gathers there, and hangs in the gap. The liquid is held against gravity. This is a response to the field, not a permanent magnet. Remove the field, and random orientations cancel the bulk spin magnetisation. Nitrogen, with every spin paired, pours straight past. Same magnet. Two liquids. Molecules that differ by just two electrons. The electronic occupations explain their different responses. Bond order and magnetism, from one diagram. One fair note. Advanced valence bond calculations can also describe oxygen's unpaired electrons. It's the simple Lewis pairing picture that can't.
