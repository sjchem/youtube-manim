Let's lift these maps into three dimensions. Two hydrogen nuclei, each wrapped in a one s orbital. Bring them close, in phase. The two spheres merge into a single cloud, fattest between the nuclei. This is sigma one s, the bonding orbital. Sigma, because it looks the same from every angle around the bond axis. Spin it, and nothing changes. Sigma names this symmetry, not its energy. Now bring them together out of phase. This time the clouds refuse to merge. A flat plane between the nuclei, where psi is exactly zero, separates two lobes of opposite sign. Density can still exist in both outer lobes. This is sigma star one s. The star means antibonding. Side by side: density between the nuclei, or a node between the nuclei. The colours are phase; brightness suggests density. Density between the positive nuclei attracts both. That helps stabilise the bond, although the full energy also includes electron motion and every electrical interaction. In the antibonding state, the node changes that distribution and the energy is higher. A node is not a wall pushing the atoms apart. Shape is only half the story. The other half is energy. An empty orbital contributes no electron occupancy.
