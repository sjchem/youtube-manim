Stand two p orbitals side by side, parallel, both at right angles to the bond axis. In phase, the upper lobes merge, and so do the lower ones. The result is a pi orbital. No density sits on the axis itself. Instead, it forms two long clouds, one above the bond and one below, with a nodal plane slicing through both nuclei. Out of phase, each cloud breaks apart. A second node cuts between the nuclei. Pi star. Sideways overlap is weaker than head-on overlap. So pi orbitals split less than sigma orbitals. Now the crucial part. Each atom has a second sideways p orbital, turned ninety degrees. It makes its own pi orbital, exactly the same shape, just rotated around the axis. Two pi orbitals with exactly the same energy. Chemists call them degenerate. Two orbitals, not two halves of one. And the same goes for pi star. Two of them, side by side, at the same energy. Keep your eye on that pair of pi star orbitals. That's where oxygen's secret is hiding. Sigma, sigma star, two pi, two pi star. Six p orbitals in, six molecular orbitals out.
