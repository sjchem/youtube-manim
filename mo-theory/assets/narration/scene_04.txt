This idea has a name. Linear combination of atomic orbitals. L C A O, for short. Start with two equivalent atoms. Call the orbital on atom A, phi A, and the one on atom B, phi B. One molecular orbital is their sum. The other is their difference. Both combinations describe possible molecular states. The proportional sign means we've left out a scaling factor, which keeps each orbital properly normalised. The total probability must stay one. But be careful. We are adding wave functions, not electric charges. What we can measure is where the electron is likely to be. That comes from the squared magnitude of psi, written as absolute psi squared. Square the sum, and electron density gathers between the nuclei. Compare the bright region at the centre. Square the difference, and the density is pushed outward, with an empty plane in the middle. The node has zero probability density. Here are both orbitals as maps. Blue where psi is positive, rose where it's negative. The colours show phase, not charge. Both coloured regions can contain electron density. And notice what just happened. Two atomic orbitals went in. Two molecular orbitals came out. Not one. Not three. Two. An orbital is not an electron. The rule scales. Combine four atomic orbitals, and you get four molecular orbitals. Combine N, and you get N. This is an approximation. Real molecules need more basis functions, and electrons that repel each other. But within the basis we choose, the count is exact. One combination helps hold the atoms together. The other works against it.
