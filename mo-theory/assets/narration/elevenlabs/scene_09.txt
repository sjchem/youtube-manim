[warm, conversational delivery] Let's try helium. Two helium atoms, with two electrons each. Four electrons in total. Their shells are already full. The first two fill sigma one s, just like hydrogen. But sigma one s is now full. The next two electrons have only one place left to go. Up, into the antibonding orbital. The lower orbital cannot hold more. Now count. Two bonding, two antibonding. Two minus two, over two. We count electrons, not the number of lines. [short pause] [surprised] Bond order zero. The conventional covalent contribution is zero here. We added electrons, and the bond disappeared. The antibonding pair cancels everything the bonding pair contributed. Count both sets before deciding whether bonding wins. But bond order is not a complete energy calculation. At ordinary separations, filled helium shells repel rather than forming a conventional covalent bond. Our balance is only a counting aid. That's why ordinary helium is a gas of single atoms. A very weak dimer is an exception we'll return to. More electrons do not automatically mean more bonding. It depends on which orbitals they fill. The highest occupied level is the key. [curious] Here's a test. Remove one electron from He two. Which one goes? Choose before we show the answer. The highest one, from the antibonding orbital. Two bonding, one antibonding. Bond order one half. He two plus can exist, and it does. Its unpaired electron also predicts paramagnetism. Line them up. H two plus, one half. H two, one. He two plus, one half. He two, zero. The same rule gives these different results. [short pause] [thoughtful] One honest footnote. In extremely cold helium, two atoms can just barely cling together, more than fifty times farther apart than the atoms in hydrogen. That's a far weaker attraction, called dispersion, which this simple counting leaves out. Bond order zero means no covalent bond, not no attraction at all.
