[warm, conversational delivery] Hydrogen and helium only use s orbitals. Most of chemistry also needs p orbitals. A p orbital has two lobes of opposite phase, with a node through the nucleus. Point two of them straight at each other, along the bond axis. Match the phases, so that the lobes that meet have the same sign, and bring them together. The facing lobes merge into one thick cloud between the nuclei. Behind each nucleus, a small lobe of the opposite sign remains. One orbital can contain several connected regions. This is sigma two p. Turn it around the bond axis, and again, it looks the same from every side. That's what sigma means. [curious] Now flip one phase, and the facing lobes cancel instead. A node splits the bond down the middle. That's sigma star two p. Head-on overlap is strong, so these two orbitals split far apart in energy. But each atom has three p orbitals, at right angles to each other. Only one of them can point along the bond. [short pause] [curious] What happens to the other two, the ones that stand side by side? They can't meet head-on. They can only overlap sideways. Their orientation changes the overlap and the resulting shape.
