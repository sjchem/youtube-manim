On the right, for oxygen and fluorine, sigma two p drops below the pi pair. [curious] Why would the order change? Not because chemists enjoy memorising two diagrams. There is a physical reason. Only orbitals with compatible symmetry can mix. Here, the bonding sigma two s and sigma two p combinations have the required symmetry to interact. When their energies are close, they push on each other. The lower one is pushed down, and the upper one is pushed up. This is called s p mixing. And how hard they push depends on the gap between the atom's own two s and two p energies. Across the period, that gap grows. Small for boron. Larger for nitrogen. Larger still for oxygen and fluorine. [warmly] Let's drive the gap and watch. With a large gap, mixing is weak, and sigma two p stays low, below pi. Oxygen's order. The pi pair is our reference for comparison. Shrink the gap. The push gets stronger, and sigma two p climbs, until it crosses above the pi pair. Nitrogen's order. Its energy rises relative to the unchanged pi pair. The crossing happens between nitrogen and oxygen. This sketch connects two different molecular orderings. [short pause] [thoughtful] This is a simplified picture. Real orbital energies come from careful calculations and from spectroscopy. But the reason for the swap is this push between orbitals of the same symmetry. So before filling a diagram, choose the right ordering: boron to nitrogen on one side, oxygen and fluorine on the other. Then fill it, from the bottom up.
