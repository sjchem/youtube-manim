So far we have seen two extremes. Perfect sharing in hydrogen. Near-total transfer in salt. What lies in between? Remember electronegativity from the last video: how strongly an atom in a bond pulls shared electrons towards itself. In hydrogen, both atoms are identical. Same pull, so the sharing is perfectly symmetric. Now hydrogen chloride. Chlorine's electronegativity is about three point two; hydrogen's is about two point two. Chlorine pulls harder, and the shared cloud shifts towards it. The chlorine end becomes slightly negative, and the hydrogen end slightly positive. We write delta minus and delta plus: partial charges. This is a polar covalent bond. Still shared, but unequally. Make the difference larger still. Sodium and chlorine differ by more than two. Now the cloud slides almost entirely onto chlorine. The sharing is so lopsided that ions describe it better. So there are not two separate boxes, covalent and ionic. There is a continuum. Equal sharing at one end. Unequal sharing in the middle. Mostly ionic at the other end. The bar orders examples by electronegativity difference. It is a guide, not a numerical scale of ionic character. Even in table salt there is a little sharing left, and even hydrogen chloride has some ionic character. Polar bonds can make whole molecules polar too, but that also depends on their shape. Two polar bonds can even cancel out. We will save that for next time.
