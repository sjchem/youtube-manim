[warm, conversational delivery] Valence bond theory describes many bonds through overlapping atomic orbitals and paired electron spins. In hydrogen, each atom brings one electron. Overlap and spin pairing can produce a lower-energy state; overlap alone does not guarantee a bond. Two hydrogen one s orbitals. These spin arrows are quantum labels, not tiny spinning balls. Bring them together. Where they overlap, electron density builds up between the nuclei. Our illustrative density surfaces join into a shared cloud along the bond axis. They show the idea, not an exact molecular calculation. [curious] Now turn the molecule around that line. From every angle it looks the same: it is symmetric around the bond axis. [thoughtful] A bond like this is called a sigma bond. Head-on overlap, with density concentrated right on the axis. But many atoms bond using p orbitals. Each p orbital has two lobes, pointing in opposite directions. There are three of them, at right angles to each other: along x, along y, and along z. Take chlorine. Each atom has one half-filled three p orbital. Line the two up end to end, along the bond axis. They meet head-on. The facing lobes overlap, and density builds up on the axis between the nuclei. Turn it around the axis again. Same symmetry, same kind of bond. This is also a sigma bond. Head-on overlap gives a sigma bond, whether the orbitals are s or p. [curious] But what if two p orbitals meet side by side? They cannot point at each other. Yet they can still overlap, above and below the line between them.
