[warm, conversational delivery] The octet rule works beautifully for carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine. [thoughtful] But it is a pattern, not a law. Hydrogen is the first exception. Its only shell holds just two electrons, so it is complete with a duet. Boron trifluoride. Boron brings only three valence electrons and forms three bonds. Count around boron: six, not eight. Boron trifluoride is stable despite that count. It can accept a lone pair from another species, acting as a Lewis acid. Go the other way. In sulfur hexafluoride, six fluorine atoms surround one sulfur. The simple Lewis count puts twelve electrons around it. This is electron counting, not twelve electrons packed into sulfur's shell. The drawing shows connections; sulfur hexafluoride's actual shape is octahedral. Even when the count works, the picture can mislead. Our Lewis structure of oxygen shows every electron paired. [curious] Yet liquid oxygen sticks to a magnet. That magnetism comes from unpaired electrons, which this simple drawing cannot show. Explaining it needs molecular orbital theory, a story for another day. [warmly] The octet rule is a useful shortcut. Stability comes from the full electronic arrangement and energy, not from counting to eight. Reaching a lower-energy arrangement can also require crossing an energy barrier.
