[warm, conversational delivery] Let's put the three kinds of bonding side by side. Covalent: electron density shared between neighbouring nuclei, attracting both. Ionic: electrons transferred, and a lattice of opposite charges holding itself together. Metallic: electrons delocalised through the whole solid, gluing a lattice of positive cores. [curious] They look completely different. But let's ask the same question of each one, the question we started with. Plot the energy as the atoms or ions come together. Each one has a valley. Different depths, different shapes, but the same story. Separated particles up here, a stable arrangement down there. None of these bonds is a tiny physical hook. They are arrangements of nuclei and electrons, shaped by electromagnetism and quantum mechanics. The stable structures are simply the ones that reach a low enough energy. Periodic trends help us predict the balance. Similar electronegativities often favour sharing; large differences suggest ionic character. Metallic behaviour involves extended electronic states. These are guides, not rigid boundaries. The octet rule, Lewis dots, sigma and pi bonds, partial charges: they are all tools for describing where the electrons go. [short pause] [thoughtful] Underneath every one of them is a single idea. Atoms stick together when, together, they have lower energy than apart. [warmly] That is the chemical bond. But this valley only tells us how far apart two atoms sit. It says nothing yet about the angles between bonds, and that is where molecules get their shapes.
