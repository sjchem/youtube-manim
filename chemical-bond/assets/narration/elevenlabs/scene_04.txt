[warm, conversational delivery] Here is a schematic energy curve for two hydrogen atoms, plotted against the distance between their nuclei. Far to the right, the atoms barely notice each other. Call that energy zero: the separated atoms. As they approach, the attractions win. The total energy falls, and the gold dot slides downhill. Too close, and repulsion takes over. The energy shoots up, like a wall. [thoughtful] Between the slope and the wall there is a valley. At its very bottom sits the lowest energy this pair can reach. For hydrogen, that bottom is at about seventy-four picometres. We call it the bond length: the equilibrium distance. This Morse curve is a model, not a full quantum calculation. Its push and pull terms illustrate how a minimum appears; they are not separate measured forces. [curious] Now let's give that valley one more dimension. Hold one atom fixed in the centre, and let the other wander across a plane. The height of this surface is the energy at every position. The valley becomes a circular moat at the bond length. These markers settle by losing energy; a real isolated pair cannot simply stop without releasing energy. Slice through the middle, and there is our curve again. [short pause] [thoughtful] A bond is not a hook. It is the bottom of an energy valley, and the rest of this story is built on that idea.
