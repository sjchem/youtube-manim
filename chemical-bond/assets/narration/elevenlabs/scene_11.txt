[warm, conversational delivery] In solid salt, ions form an extended structure. Sodium and chloride ions repeat in a three-dimensional lattice. Positive, negative, positive, negative, in every direction. This is the rock salt lattice. [curious] Pick one sodium ion. Six chloride ions touch it: above, below, left, right, front and back. Six attractions. A little farther out sit twelve sodium ions. Like charges. Twelve repulsions. Then eight more chlorides, attracting again. And on and on, through the whole crystal. [thoughtful] Sum the electrostatic attractions and repulsions, and attraction wins. Per ion pair, that contribution is about one point seven five times the single-pair value at the same distance. From gaseous ions, lattice formation releases about seven hundred and eighty-seven kilojoules per mole. Our overall balance starts from gaseous atoms. Solid salt contains no separate sodium chloride molecules. Each sodium ion has six equivalent nearest chloride neighbours, in an extended lattice. With doubly charged ions, as in magnesium oxide, the lattice energy is almost five times larger. It melts above two thousand eight hundred degrees Celsius. [short pause] [warmly] Ionic bonding is not a single handoff. It is a whole crystal settling into a deep energy valley.
