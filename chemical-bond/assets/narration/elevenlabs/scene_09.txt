[warm, conversational delivery] Here are two p orbitals, standing parallel to each other, side by side. Bring the atoms close, and the upper lobes overlap above the axis. The lower lobes overlap below it. [thoughtful] The result is a pi bond: two regions of shared density, one above the bond axis and one below. A nodal plane contains the bond axis. In this pi contribution, electron density is zero throughout that plane. For comparable orbitals, sideways overlap is usually smaller. That often makes a pi contribution weaker than a sigma one. In the ordinary double and triple bonds we are building, pi bonding adds to a sigma framework. [warmly] Now we can read single, double and triple bonds in a new way. Hydrogen: one sigma bond. Nothing else. Oxygen, in this simple picture. One pair of p orbitals meets head-on, forming a sigma bond. Another pair stands side by side, forming a pi bond. Sigma plus pi: a double bond. Nitrogen: one sigma bond along the axis, plus two pi bonds, at right angles to each other. Sigma plus two pi: a triple bond. So the three bonds of nitrogen are not three identical sticks. One sigma bond, wrapped in two pi clouds. That is why a double bond is not simply twice a single bond, and a triple bond is not simply three times.
