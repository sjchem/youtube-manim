Let's return to the energy valley. Every bond has one, and two numbers describe it. The position of the bottom is the bond length: the distance between the nuclei at minimum energy. A deeper energy well means more energy to separate the particles. Our schematic curves compare approximate gas-phase bond enthalpies. A deep valley means a strong bond. A shallow one, a weak bond. And a bond is never frozen at the bottom. The atoms vibrate back and forth around the equilibrium distance. Stretch the bond, and you must supply energy. Compress it too much, and repulsion pushes back. A chemical bond is not a rigid stick. It behaves more like a spring. Now compare carbon with carbon. A single bond, a double bond, a triple bond. The single bond is one hundred and fifty-four picometres long, and takes about three hundred and forty-eight kilojoules per mole to break. The double bond: one hundred and thirty-four picometres, about six hundred and fourteen. The triple bond: one hundred and twenty picometres, about eight hundred and thirty-nine. More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer and bind them more strongly. The valleys get narrower and deeper. These averages are not separate sigma and pi energies. Multiple bonds are stronger, but not simple multiples. So is shorter always stronger? Not necessarily. The fluorine bond is shorter than the chlorine bond: one hundred and forty-one picometres against one hundred and ninety-nine. Yet the fluorine bond is weaker. About one hundred and fifty-nine kilojoules per mole, against two hundred and forty-three. Fluorine atoms are so small that their lone pairs crowd each other, and that repulsion weakens the bond. For related bonds between the same elements, higher bond order commonly means shorter and stronger. Across different elements, compare the whole electronic structure. Which brings us back to that famous rule of eight, and to the places where it breaks.
