Two hydrogen atoms again. Each one brings a single electron. As they approach, the two clouds merge. Both electrons now spread over both nuclei. Look where the density builds up: between the nuclei, right where it is attracted by both of them at once. That shared electron density is the covalent bond. Two atoms, sharing a pair of electrons. Chemists draw it as a line. Or, in Lewis's notation, as a pair of dots between the two symbols. But remember what the dots stand for. Not two little balls parked between the atoms, but two electrons, spread through that whole cloud. For hydrogen's ground-state bond, the electrons form a spin singlet. In the orbital picture, two electrons occupy one bonding orbital with opposite spins. Now chlorine. Each chlorine atom has seven valence electrons: three pairs, and one lonely single. The two singles pair up between the atoms. One shared pair: a single bond. The other six valence electrons are represented as three lone pairs on each chlorine. They are not the shared bonding pair. Count around either chlorine: six of its own in lone pairs, plus the two it shares. Eight. But atoms can share more than one pair. Oxygen has six valence electrons, so each oxygen atom is two short. Two oxygen atoms share two pairs. A double bond. Nitrogen has five, so two nitrogen atoms share three pairs. A triple bond, one of the strongest bonds in chemistry. One shared pair, two, three. Chemists call this the bond order. But here is a puzzle. If both pairs of a double bond sit between the same two atoms, are they really the same? Where exactly do they go? To answer that, we need a closer look at the shapes of the orbitals themselves, and at how they overlap.
