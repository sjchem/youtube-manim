In earlier episodes we built atoms shell by shell. Here is carbon: two inner electrons, and four outer ones. When atoms meet, the outer electrons do most of the interacting. We call them valence electrons, and they do the chemistry. The inner electrons sit deep, held tightly by the nucleus. In bonding, they mostly come along for the ride. Hydrogen has one valence electron. Carbon has four. Oxygen, six. Chlorine, seven. Gilbert Lewis had a beautifully simple idea: write the symbol, and draw the valence electrons as dots around it. One dot for hydrogen. Four for carbon. Six for oxygen, and seven for chlorine, placed singly first, then in pairs. Now look at a pattern. Neon and argon, with eight valence electrons, barely react at all. And in their compounds, many atoms end up surrounded by eight electrons, counting the shared ones. This is the octet rule. It is a powerful bookkeeping tool. It predicts a huge number of structures correctly. But careful. A full outer shell is often a favourable arrangement, but atoms do not bond in order to collect eight electrons. The octet is a pattern we notice in the results. The cause is still energy.
