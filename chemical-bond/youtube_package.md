## Description

Two hydrogen atoms: the nuclei repel, the electrons repel — and yet they settle
into a molecule at exactly one distance. Why don't they fly apart, and why don't
they collapse?

Instead of memorising "ionic vs covalent" rules, this video builds chemical
bonding from electrons, nuclei and energy. We trace the energy of two atoms as
they approach and turn it into a 3D energy landscape; see why breaking a bond
always costs energy and forming one releases it; draw Lewis structures; watch
orbitals overlap in 3D to form sigma and pi bonds (valence bond theory); check
the honest energy ledger for sodium chloride (the electron transfer alone is
uphill — the crystal pays the bill); follow a shared electron cloud as it leans
from covalent to polar to ionic; see why metals bend while salt shatters;
compare bond lengths and strengths (and why the shorter F–F bond is weaker than
Cl–Cl); and find where the octet rule breaks. Everything comes back to one idea:
atoms stick together when, together, they have lower energy than apart.

This is Part 1 of the Chemical Bonding series and follows "Periodic Trends,
Explained Visually". Next: Molecular Geometry, Explained Visually.

Numbers on screen come from published data: NIST (bond lengths, ionization
energy, electron affinity), the CRC Handbook (bond dissociation and lattice
energies), and standard textbook averages for carbon–carbon bonds. Electron
clouds and orbital surfaces are illustrative models (LCAO densities, seeded
probability samples), labelled as such; the energy curve is a schematic Morse model, with the energy scale calibrated
to an approximate gas-phase bond enthalpy.
