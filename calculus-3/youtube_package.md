## Description

If you never see a function — only how fast it changes — can calculus rebuild it?

That single question turns integration into something much bigger than "area under a curve". It becomes a way to reconstruct motion, growth, cooling, oscillation, and entire physical systems.

This is Part 3 of the Visual Calculus series. A compact car recap distinguishes displacement from absolute position and identifies the constant of integration as genuinely missing starting information. Then we meet an equation that never names a function at all — dy/dx = y — and learn to read it as a field of directions filling the plane. Release a point into that field and a solution curve emerges tangent to its local segments.

From there: why e appears wherever growth is proportional to size; how one minus sign turns growth into decay; why a cup of coffee cools fast and then slowly; how separation preserves its equilibrium solution; why initial data decide which world the law is acting in; how F = ma becomes a differential equation; how an ideal spring turns it into oscillation; how spinning and slicing a semicircle gives 4π/3; and finally, how repeated numerical steps turn a local gravitational rule into a planetary orbit.

## Pinned comment

The three visual claims this episode rests on, in one place:
1. Integrating velocity reconstructs displacement; a starting value is still needed for absolute position.
2. A differential equation constrains local change, and a slope field makes that rule visible before solving.
3. The unifying identity is y(t) = y₀ + ∫f(y(τ),τ)dτ: future state equals starting state plus accumulated change.
