Why Electrons Don’t Orbit the Nucleus

Target: 23–27 minutes
Core question: If electrons aren't orbiting the nucleus like planets, where are they?

Core idea:
The electron does not have a classical trajectory around the nucleus. Its wave-like nature leads to allowed quantum states, and those states determine where an electron is likely to be found, orbital shapes, electron configurations—and ultimately chemistry itself.

Position poorly defined.

Then return to Bohr orbit.

A trajectory requires knowing:

position + velocity

Now everything becomes positive.

Explain:

$$ |\psi|^2 $$

tells us the probability density for detecting the electron at a location.

The textbook explicitly distinguishes the wave function from the physically meaningful probability density \(|\psi|^2\).

The 5 Visual Moments I Would Spend the Most Time On

If animation time is limited, make these exceptional:

1s → 2s → p → d orbital transformation in 3D

Those five scenes alone can make the video feel unlike a normal high-school chemistry lesson.

One Scientific Language Rule

I would be careful with:

“The electron is a cloud.”

Instead say:

“The cloud represents the probability distribution for detecting the electron.”

Similarly, don't say uncertainty happens simply because our instruments aren't good enough. In quantum mechanics the limitation is intrinsic to the quantum state; the wave-packet animation makes this much clearer.

That single choice will make the video feel more sophisticated while remaining understandable.

What NOT to Spend Time On

For this video I would avoid:

deriving Schrödinger's equation
detailed Fourier mathematics behind uncertainty
calculating de Broglie wavelengths repeatedly
memorizing all five d-orbital names
detailed radial probability equations
long node-count calculations
lengthy shielding calculations
deriving the \(n+l\) rule
every electronic configuration up to krypton
long exchange-energy explanation for Cr/Cu

The chapter contains much more detail, but your video's job should be to give viewers the mental model first.
