Jump to carbon. Its one s and two s subshells are filled, leaving two electrons for the three equal-energy p orbitals. Here is the first. Where should the second go: alongside it, or into another orbital?

Both choices can respect Pauli exclusion. But for carbon's ground state, the lower-energy arrangement occupies separate p orbitals with parallel spins. We have met Hund's first rule through the choice it explains.

This preference comes from electron interactions and quantum symmetry. The boxes count states; they are not little rooms inside the atom.

Now watch what happens when the choice runs out. Oxygen has eight electrons. Four fill one s and two s. Three of the remaining four go singly into the three p orbitals, one each, exactly as Hund's rule prefers.

The fourth has nowhere empty left to go. So it pairs, with opposite spin, in an orbital that already has an electron. Hund's rule is not a ban on pairing; it is a preference for spreading out while spreading out is still possible. And because all three p orbitals have the same energy here, it makes no difference which one takes the pair.

Now complete the set in one step, arriving at neon. Each p orbital holds an opposite-spin pair. Three spatial orbitals, with two spin states each, give six electrons in a full p subshell. The capacity follows from the quantum labels and exclusion.

These arrows record occupancy, not permanent identities for individual electrons. With the outer shell complete, what changes when we add one more electron?
