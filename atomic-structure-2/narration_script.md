# Why Electrons Don’t Orbit the Nucleus

Part 2 · spoken narration

## Scene 01 — Where is the electron? — 0:00–0:40

Here is a very simple question: where is this electron?
Our little planetary model seems to give an obvious answer: right here.

And how fast is it moving?
Draw a velocity arrow, and the picture seems complete.

But those two apparently harmless answers are about to break this entire picture of the atom.

The problem is not that we drew the orbit at the wrong size.
The problem is the orbit itself.

If electrons do not travel around the nucleus like planets…
then what does it actually mean for an electron to be somewhere inside an atom?

## Scene 02 — What survived the orbit — 0:40–1:30

In the previous video, experiments forced us to rebuild the atom. Scattering revealed a tiny nucleus. Hydrogen's bright lines revealed a small set of allowed energy changes.

Bohr connected those clues. His model predicted hydrogen's main energy levels remarkably well. But a successful energy calculation did not prove that the electron followed his circular track.

Watch what we keep. The nucleus remains. The allowed energies remain. The spectrum remains. The route around the nucleus becomes a question mark.

We now need a description that earns those energies, instead of attaching them to a picture that cannot survive. The first clue comes from light.

## Scene 03 — Everything is fixed — 1:30–3:10

Before we follow that light, look at what the model we are leaving actually promised. It is worth seeing while it is still convincing.

Bohr's hydrogen electron travels on fixed circular orbits, labelled n equals one, two, three. School shell diagrams often add a capacity rule: two n squared, giving two, eight, eighteen, thirty-two. Keep that rule in mind, but separate its origin: it comes from quantum states and spin, not Bohr's single-electron orbit.

The shells sit at fixed distances. In hydrogen, the first is about zero point five three angstroms from the nucleus. The second is about two point one two.

Each shell carries a fixed energy: minus thirteen point six electronvolts for the first, minus three point four for the second, following minus thirteen point six over n squared. Those numbers matched hydrogen's measured spectrum, which is why the model was taken seriously.

And the electron moves at a fixed speed. In the first shell of hydrogen, about two point one nine million metres per second.

A fixed radius, energy and speed: a completely specified classical orbit. Beside it, a shell capacity rule still waiting for an explanation.

Keep those numbers. We will discover which predictions survive, and which physical picture has to change.

Because here is what this picture cannot do. Bring a second atom close to the first. Nothing in a table of fixed orbits says whether these two should join together, or why they would.

## Scene 04 — Momentum becomes wavelength — 3:10–5:40

Light spreads and interferes like a wave, yet a detector records individual arrivals: photons. Louis de Broglie asked whether the connection could work the other way. Could a particle of matter have a wavelength?

His relation is lambda equals h divided by p. Here h is Planck's constant, and p is momentum. For a slow electron, compared with light, momentum is approximately its mass times its speed.

Take an electron moving at one million metres per second. Its wavelength is about zero point seven three nanometres. Double the speed: momentum doubles, and the wavelength halves to about zero point three six nanometres. Watch the bracket shrink with the calculated number.

The curve represents quantum amplitude. It does not show an electron travelling along a wavy road.

Now apply the same relation to something you could hold. A cricket ball has a mass of about one hundred and sixty grams. Send it down the pitch at forty metres per second. Its momentum is enormous by atomic standards, so its wavelength comes out at about one times ten to the minus thirty-four metres.

It is worth seeing how small that is. On this logarithmic scale an atom sits near ten to the minus ten metres, a nucleus near ten to the minus fifteen. The ball's wavelength is nineteen further powers of ten below the nucleus. Diffraction of an ordinary cricket ball is far beyond experimental resolution. Its wave nature is not absent; its wavelength is extraordinarily small.

Now spend one of the numbers we banked. Bohr gave the first-shell electron a speed of two point one nine million metres per second. Put that into the same relation, and the wavelength is about three point three angstroms. That is not small. That is the size of the atom itself.

So send an electron beam through a crystal, whose atoms are spaced at about that distance. Each atom contributes a scattering amplitude, A sub j. Add those amplitudes, then square the magnitude to predict intensity. Waves reinforce in some directions and cancel in others. Individual detections build the bright bands and gaps of a diffraction pattern.

But a crystal is a crowd of scatterers. What happens if we send the electrons through one at a time?

## Scene 05 — One electron at a time — 5:40–7:10

Here is the cleanest version of that experiment. A source, a barrier with two narrow openings, and a screen behind it.

First try narrow streams of tiny classical balls. With this simple geometry, each opening produces a strip of arrivals. Open both and you add the two distributions. There is no interference term.

Now fire electrons instead. Not a beam — one electron at a time, with a long pause between each.

Every one of them arrives as a single point. One flash, at one place. Nothing about an individual arrival looks like a wave.

But let them accumulate. After a few hundred, the screen is not showing two strips. It is showing a series of bands, separated by gaps where almost nothing lands.

Those gaps are the surprising part. There are places on that screen which electrons reach when one slit is open and stop reaching when both are. Opening a second route removes arrivals. That is interference.

The two routes contribute amplitudes, A one and A two. Add them before squaring, and they can reinforce or cancel. Now start a fresh run with a detector that records which slit. Distinguishable paths lose that interference term: their probabilities add, leaving a smooth diffraction envelope.

The physical detector interaction matters, even if nobody reads its record.

## Scene 06 — Waves that fit — 7:10–8:25

Take the old circle for one last experiment with an idea. Wrap a wave around it. When the ends disagree, there is no single consistent wave at the join. Change the wavelength until the wave meets itself smoothly. Now a whole number of wavelengths fits: four, five, six, and nothing in between.

Now take the smallest pattern of all — one wavelength, closing on itself — and put in the numbers we already have.

The wavelength we calculated for the first-shell electron was three point three two four nine angstroms. The circumference of Bohr's first orbit is three point three two four nine angstroms. The same number.

Write the condition out. Two pi r equals n lambda. Substitute lambda equals h over m v, and you get m v r equals n h over two pi.

That recovers Bohr's quantisation rule within this ring model. It shows how a wave boundary condition can select discrete possibilities.

A hydrogen orbital is a three-dimensional wave, not this ring. Its angular momentum will follow a different rule. First, what does a wave mean for position?

## Scene 07 — The price of locating a wave — 8:25–11:45

Go back to the numbers we banked. Bohr placed the first-shell electron at a radius of zero point five three angstroms, travelling at two point one nine million metres per second. Multiply that speed by the electron's mass and you have its momentum, about one point nine nine times ten to the minus twenty-four kilogram metres per second.

Radius and speed are only magnitudes. A classical trajectory also specifies the electron's exact position and momentum vectors at each instant. It is that complete path we must reconsider.

Here is the argument most people meet first. To see an electron you must bounce something off it. A short-wavelength photon locates it sharply, but because photon momentum is h over lambda, short wavelengths carry large momentum, so it strikes hard and the momentum afterwards is not what it was. A long-wavelength photon kicks gently, but the position is blurred.

That story blames the apparatus, but the uncertainty relation describes the state itself. Set the microscope aside and look at the wave, with no measurement taking place.

De Broglie's relation can also be written p equals h-bar times k. Here h-bar is h divided by two pi, and k is two pi divided by wavelength. Shorter wavelengths therefore carry greater momentum.

Look at this long, regular wave. It has a definite wavelength and momentum, but no small region stands out as its location. To concentrate the wave, add neighbouring wavelengths. They reinforce in one region and cancel elsewhere. A wave packet emerges.

The more tightly we localize the packet, the wider the collection of wavelengths we need. Through de Broglie's relation, that becomes a wider spread of momenta. Watch the position and momentum distributions change together.

Their widths are standard deviations across repeated preparations of the same state — statistical spreads, not the errors of a faulty ruler. Squeeze the position spread, and the momentum spread grows.

Werner Heisenberg's uncertainty principle puts a precise bound on this trade: delta x times delta p is at least h-bar divided by two.

Give an electron a position spread of one hundred picometres, an atomic distance. The minimum momentum spread is about five point two seven times ten to the minus twenty-five kilogram metres per second, and our Gaussian packet reaches it. Halve the position spread, and that minimum doubles.

This limit belongs to the quantum state itself. Better equipment cannot prepare both quantities with arbitrarily narrow spreads.

A classical orbit assigns exact position and momentum at each instant. A quantum state cannot have zero spread in both a position coordinate and its corresponding momentum component. Both can have finite spreads; uncertainty does not mean we can measure only one quantity. We need a wave state and predictions for measurements.

But uncertainty does not tell us which atomic states are allowed. For that, we need an equation that includes the electron's energy and its attraction to the nucleus.

## Scene 08 — Which waves can exist? — 11:45–14:05

Which wave states can exist around a nucleus? Start with a string fixed at both ends. A fundamental pattern fits. Another fits with one interior node; another with two. The boundary conditions select the stationary patterns. Other motions can combine these modes.

An atom has no string and no rigid outer wall. The analogy supplies a question, not an atomic model: what determines which wave patterns can exist?

Erwin Schrödinger supplied an equation for the wave function. In its stationary form, H acting on psi equals E times psi. Expand H, and two contributions appear: kinetic energy, involving how the wave function curves through space, and potential energy, describing the interaction.

For hydrogen, the potential is the electrical attraction between electron and proton. Its negative sign reflects attraction, with zero energy chosen for particles separated infinitely far apart. Acceptable bound solutions must also have a finite total probability that we can normalize.

Now watch what solving the equation gives us. The one s state has a spherical probability distribution and an energy of about minus thirteen point six electronvolts. The hydrogen energies follow minus thirteen point six divided by n squared, in this nonrelativistic approximation.

Choose the two p solution. The pattern has two lobes, and its energy is about minus three point four electronvolts. The spatial state and its energy arrive together. We recover Bohr's main hydrogen energies without assigning an electron a circular route.

A stationary state's probability density stays fixed in time, although its wave function has a changing overall phase. The camera rotation lets us inspect the shape; it is not motion of the electron density.

There is a connection to de Broglie here too. A definite wave number carries momentum, and kinetic energy involves momentum squared. In the wave equation, spatial derivatives represent that contribution. Uncertainty constrains the spreads; Schrödinger's equation determines the states. Next, what does psi predict when we detect an electron?

## Scene 09 — From amplitude to detections — 14:05–17:00

This picture uses two colors for opposite signs of a real spatial wave function. Both colors describe the same kind of electron. They do not mean positive and negative electric charge. And the height of this wave is not itself a probability.

A wave function can have sign, and more generally complex phase. Those features matter when amplitudes interfere. A negative amplitude is perfectly sensible. A negative probability is not. So how do we get from the wave function to predictions about detections?

Take its absolute value squared. The result is the probability density, written as the modulus of psi squared. Both signs produce nonnegative density. Where the amplitude is large in magnitude, the density is large. Where the wave function vanishes, the density vanishes too.

Density is not the probability of a dimensionless point. To predict a probability, collect the density over a small region of space. For a normalized state, collecting it over all space gives one. The electron must be detected somewhere if the ideal position measurement succeeds.

Here is an analogy for collecting statistics. Photograph one cat at random times over a month. Many photographs find it near a warm doorway; fewer find it farther away. One photograph records a position. Many photographs estimate a distribution. The analogy stops there: a cat has an ordinary trajectory between photographs. An orbital does not supply a hidden classical route for an electron.

Now prepare hydrogen in the same state again and again. Each run contributes one position measurement. One dot appears. Then another run, another dot. No individual detection traces out the cloud. The distribution only becomes visible as many independently prepared trials accumulate.

The dots here are repeated outcomes for one electron, not hundreds of electrons simultaneously orbiting this nucleus. Fade the individual records into a smooth density map. It describes where further trials are more or less likely to produce detections.

The cloud is therefore not a photograph of a fuzzy little object. It is a prediction map extracted from a quantum state. The same state can also predict a momentum distribution. Its position density alone does not contain every piece of information in psi.

We can finally name the replacement for the old circular path. It is an orbital, and that small change of word marks a very large change of idea.

## Scene 10 — A state replaces a path — 17:00–18:10

An orbit is a path. At a given moment, the classical object occupies a point on that path and has a velocity along it.

An atomic orbital is a one-electron spatial wave function. The cloud we use to picture it is a representation of its probability density. It does not tell us a route between successive detections.

Watch the tracking marker disappear while the distribution remains. Asking where we might detect the electron still makes sense. Asking which little segment of this circular road it secretly occupies is no longer what the orbital describes.

For a many-electron atom, orbitals will become part of an approximate description of a more complicated quantum state. That will still be enormously useful for chemistry.

First, stay with one electron. If these patterns come from an equation, why should every solution look like a sphere? They do not. The next patterns will make the distinction impossible to miss.

## Scene 11 — Shapes and missing probability — 18:10–21:55

The lowest-energy hydrogen orbital, one s, has a spherical probability density. Rotate the view. There is no preferred direction. Its outer haze gradually thins; there is no hard wall at the edge of the atom.

The surface now drawn encloses approximately ninety percent of the probability in our numerical model. It is a display choice. Changing that percentage moves the surface without changing the quantum state. Some probability always remains outside a finite boundary for this bound state.

Density and distance ask different questions. One s density is greatest at the nucleus. For probability in a thin spherical shell, multiply density by four pi r squared, then by its thickness.

The radial curve peaks at one Bohr radius, about zero point five three angstroms. That is the most probable radius, not an orbit. The shaded area tells a different story: only thirty-two percent lies inside that radius. Expand the sphere to about two point six six Bohr radii and it encloses ninety percent. The boundary changes; the state does not.

Move to two s. The state extends farther out, but size is not the only change. A spherical node appears. Across that radius the real wave function changes sign. At the node it is zero, so the probability density is zero too.

We cut the display open to reveal that empty-probability surface. This is not a solid shell that blocks the electron. It is a feature of the wave function. The colors on either side mark opposite signs, while both sides still contribute positive detection density.

Now change the angular pattern. Two p has two lobes separated by a nodal plane. Rotate that plane through the nucleus and the whole shape changes orientation. The familiar p x, p y, and p z drawings are three useful real orbital patterns, with the same energy in an isolated atom without an external field.

Again, the lobes are not two electrons and they are not containers. They belong to one spatial state. A detection produces one result, somewhere sampled from that state's distribution.

Now the d states, and there are five of them. Three have four lobes lying between a pair of axes: d x y, d x z and d y z. A fourth, d x squared minus y squared, has four lobes along the axes. And d z squared has two lobes with a ring around the middle. Five independent patterns, which is two l plus one when l is two. Only d z squared is a single magnetic state by itself; the other four are combinations.

The equation also counts the nodes: n minus l minus one radial nodes, and l angular nodes, giving n minus one in total. Two s has one radial node; two p has one angular node. At every node, amplitude and density vanish. These are properties of solutions, not decorations. Now we need names for those solutions.

## Scene 12 — An address for a quantum state — 21:55–26:15

Choose one of the patterns we have just seen. How would you tell someone exactly which orbital to draw, without sending the entire picture? The equation supplies a set of labels. These are the quantum numbers. We will build the address from the picture.

First choose the principal quantum number, n. One, two, three, and so on identify shells. For ideal hydrogen, n determines the main bound-state energy. Increasing n also increases the characteristic spatial scale, although no shell is a hard spherical container. In many-electron atoms, energy will depend on more than n alone.

Within a chosen shell, select the angular pattern with l. The allowed values run from zero up to n minus one. Watch why that range matters. The first shell has only l equals zero. The second adds l equals one. The third also permits l equals two.

We write s, p, d and f for l equals zero, one, two and three. These are angular families; radial structure still depends on n. And l measures something: orbital angular momentum squared is l times l plus one times h-bar squared. An s state has zero orbital angular momentum. Unlike Bohr's orbit, hydrogen one s does not circulate with angular momentum h-bar.

To organize the labels, picture houses numbered n. Each has n floors, labelled l from zero to n minus one: s, p, d, and so on. House one has one floor; house two has two; house three has three. The building illustrates a restriction already obtained from the wave equation.

And on each floor there are rooms. A room is one orbital.

Count the rooms: s has one orbital, p has three, d has five. Each floor has two l plus one. The first three houses therefore contain one; one plus three; and one plus three plus five rooms. One, four, nine: n squared spatial orbitals.

With two spin states per orbital and the Pauli restriction we will use shortly, a shell can hold two n squared electrons: two, eight, eighteen.

The school capacity rule now has a reason: counting distinct quantum states.

Remember, though, that this is an analogy. A shell is not a container and a floor is not a place. What survives is the counting.

Next comes m sub l, the orbital angular momentum projection along a chosen axis. L z equals m sub l times h-bar. Values from minus l to plus l give two l plus one states. For p, the projections are minus h-bar, zero, and plus h-bar.

For p, there are three. The familiar real x, y, and z drawings span the same three-state space. Be careful: the x and y pictures are combinations of the magnetic states, not separate literal assignments of minus one and plus one. We use the real drawings because their directional shapes are easier to see.

The address two, one, zero selects two p z: angular momentum squared is two h-bar squared, while its z projection is zero. Three labels identify this spatial orbital. The electron also has spin: m sub s equals plus or minus one half, so S z equals plus or minus h-bar over two.

The arrows represent these two spin states. They do not show a tiny charged ball physically spinning clockwise or anticlockwise. Spin is an intrinsic quantum property.

Our address is complete. Shell, angular family, magnetic state, spin projection. The labels are compact names for distinctions that the physics already required. Now, what changes when another electron joins the atom?

## Scene 13 — Other electrons change the energies — 26:15–28:45

With hydrogen, one electron feels the attraction of one nucleus. In the simplest model, states with the same principal quantum number share an energy. Two s and two p sit together.

Add electrons, and they also repel one another. Each electron's distribution changes the environment experienced by the others. Inner density partly screens the nuclear attraction.

But screening is only half of it. Compare hydrogen's three s, three p and three d radial curves to see a shape effect. Their innermost peaks sit near three quarters, three, and nine Bohr radii. Three d has much less probability near the nucleus, not none. These hydrogen curves illustrate penetration; actual many-electron orbitals contract and change shape.

Greater probability inside the screening density means stronger attraction to the nucleus. This generally lowers s relative to p, and p relative to d within a shell. Penetration helps explain the splitting alongside electron repulsion.

The energy picture therefore splits. In many-electron atoms, n alone no longer determines an orbital's energy. The angular family matters too. Our orbitals are now useful approximate building blocks of a joint many-electron state.

These shelves show a common filling guide for neutral atoms: one s, two s, two p, three s, three p, four s, then three d. Their spacing here is schematic.

And look what that ordering contains. Four s has come out below three d. If electrons fill the lowest available energy, a shell can begin before the one beneath it is finished. That is strange enough to be worth a proper answer, and we will come back to it with the atom where it first bites.

The principle underneath is to find the allowed arrangement with the lowest total energy. With that goal, and with the spin states we have introduced, we can finally build atoms one electron at a time.

## Scene 14 — Hydrogen, helium, lithium — 28:45–30:05

Start with hydrogen: one proton, one electron. Its lowest-energy state is one s. The superscript in one s, one simply counts the electron. Building from the lowest energy is the idea behind Aufbau.

Now helium adds a second electron. Can both occupy one s?

Write out their addresses. The first electron has n equals one, l equals zero, m sub l equals zero. So does the second — there is nowhere else in that subshell for it to be. Three labels, and all three are identical.

But no two electrons in an atom may share every quantum label. Three already match, so the fourth is the only one left that can differ. It has to be the spin projection: one electron plus one half, the other minus one half. Watch the second arrow reverse.

That is Pauli exclusion. One spatial orbital therefore holds two electrons with opposite spin projections. That restriction is an additional quantum principle for fermions; electrostatic repulsion alone does not explain it.

Lithium adds a third electron. One s is full, so the next electron enters two s. A new outer state opens.

## Scene 15 — Carbon and the capacity of p — 30:05–31:55

Jump to carbon. Its one s and two s subshells are filled, leaving two electrons for the three equal-energy p orbitals. Here is the first. Where should the second go: alongside it, or into another orbital?

Both choices can respect Pauli exclusion. But for carbon's ground state, the lower-energy arrangement occupies separate p orbitals with parallel spins. We have met Hund's first rule through the choice it explains.

This preference comes from electron interactions and quantum symmetry. The boxes count states; they are not little rooms inside the atom.

Now watch what happens when the choice runs out. Oxygen has eight electrons. Four fill one s and two s. Three of the remaining four go singly into the three p orbitals, one each, exactly as Hund's rule prefers.

The fourth has nowhere empty left to go. So it pairs, with opposite spin, in an orbital that already has an electron. Hund's rule is not a ban on pairing; it is a preference for spreading out while spreading out is still possible. And because all three p orbitals have the same energy here, it makes no difference which one takes the pair.

Now complete the set in one step, arriving at neon. Each p orbital holds an opposite-spin pair. Three spatial orbitals, with two spin states each, give six electrons in a full p subshell. The capacity follows from the quantum labels and exclusion.

These arrows record occupancy, not permanent identities for individual electrons. With the outer shell complete, what changes when we add one more electron?

## Scene 16 — Neon to sodium: a bridge to chemistry — 31:55–33:10

Neon's outer shell is complete. Add one proton and one electron to reach sodium. The new electron enters three s, beyond a neon-like core.

And this is the moment to settle something. You probably first met sodium as two, eight, one. We have spent this whole video building a different notation without saying that the two are the same statement.

So take the shells one at a time. The first holds two: that is one s squared. The second holds eight: two s squared, two p to the sixth. The third holds one: three s to the one. Two, eight, one. The same atom, counted twice — once by shell, once by subshell.

Now the chemistry. These density pictures are schematic, but the contrast is real. Neon is difficult to ionize and usually unreactive. Sodium gives up its outer electron much more readily, forming a positive ion in many reactions.

The difference comes from energies and interactions. Our state labels have led to electron filling, and electron filling has begun to explain chemistry.

## Scene 17 — Potassium breaks the order — 33:10–34:30

Keep going past sodium and the shelves fill in order: one s, two s, two p, three s, three p. That is eighteen electrons, and the atom is argon.

Now potassium, with nineteen. One electron is left. The third shell holds eighteen and we have put in only eight, so surely this one finishes it and enters three d?

It does not. A simple arithmetic guide predicts the order, although explaining the energy requires the interactions we just discussed.

Madelung's guide orders subshells by increasing n plus l, breaking ties with smaller n. Two p and three s both give three, so two p comes first. Three p gives four.

Now the two candidates. Three d: three plus two is five. Four s: four plus zero is four. Four is less than five.

So the nineteenth electron goes into four s. The fourth shell opens before the third is full.

The ground configuration minimizes total energy. This guide often predicts it for neutral atoms, but four s and three d energies depend on occupancy and ionization. They are not fixed shelves. The periodic table and its exceptions are our next video's story.

## Scene 18 — The answer to the opening question — 34:30–35:05

Where is the electron? Quantum mechanics gives us a state that predicts probabilities for observations. Our circular road became a wave, a detection map, and an orbital.

The allowed states now tell us how electrons can fill an atom. Fill an outer shell, then begin another, and patterns start to repeat. Those patterns shape chemistry.
