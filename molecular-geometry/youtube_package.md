## Description

Carbon dioxide and water both have three atoms. One is perfectly straight; the
other is bent at about 104.5°. Why?

This video builds molecular geometry from the electrons around a central atom.
Lewis structures turn out to be flat bookkeeping, so we lift molecules into 3D
and watch electron regions push apart — a simple repulsion model finds the
linear, trigonal planar and tetrahedral arrangements on its own. We count
regions (AXmEn and the steric number), derive 109.47° from the corners of a
cube, and see how invisible lone pairs reshape NH₃ and H₂O. The same rule
explains the five-region family (PCl₅, SF₄, ClF₃, XeF₂) and the six-region
family (SF₆, BrF₅, XeF₄, which ends up flat). Then hybridization: a dial mixes
carbon's s and p orbitals into sp, sp² and sp³ hybrids, and orthogonality of ideal equivalent hybrids
fixes their angles at cos θ = −1/n. We meet σ and π in methane and
ethene, keep VSEPR and hybridization honest about what each one does, and
finish with polarity: bond dipoles are vectors, and shape decides whether they
cancel. Finally, three challenge molecules to predict yourself.

This is Part 2 of the Chemical Bonding series, following "Chemical Bonding,
Explained Visually". Next: Molecular Orbital Theory, Explained Visually — why
is oxygen attracted to a magnet?

Measured angles and bond lengths come from gas-phase experimental structures
(NIST CCCBDB; CRC Handbook). VSEPR is a qualitative model: it predicts the
arrangement and the trend, not exact angles. Lone-pair and region lobes are
schematic; orbital shapes are polar plots of the angular part of the
wave function; dipole arrows point toward the more electronegative atom
(the chemistry convention) and their lengths are illustrative.
