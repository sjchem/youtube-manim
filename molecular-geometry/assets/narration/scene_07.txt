Now for the most important idea in this story. Start again with methane's four regions. Swap the carbon for nitrogen. Nitrogen has five valence electrons. Three go into bonds with hydrogen. The other two stay home, as a lone pair on the nitrogen. A lone pair is still a region of electron density. It still takes up space, and it still pushes. So ammonia still has four regions: three bonds and one lone pair. A X three E. The four have an approximately tetrahedral arrangement. But here is the catch. When we describe a molecule's shape, we only look at where the atoms are. Hide the lone pair, and look at the atoms alone. Nitrogen at the top, three hydrogens below. A pyramid with a triangular base. Trigonal pyramidal. So there are two descriptions. The electron geometry includes every region, lone pairs too. For ammonia, that is tetrahedral. The molecular geometry includes only the atoms. For ammonia, that is trigonal pyramidal. The lone pair is invisible in the final shape. But it decided the shape. And there is one more effect. Measure the angle between two hydrogens. It is not one hundred and nine point five. It is about one hundred and seven degrees. The bonds are squeezed slightly closer together. A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so in the VSEPR picture it spreads out close to the nitrogen, and crowds the bonds.
