Now replace one more hydrogen with a lone pair. The central atom becomes oxygen, with six valence electrons. Two of them bond with hydrogens. The other four form two lone pairs. Count the regions: two bonds, two lone pairs. Four again. A X two E two. So the electron geometry is still tetrahedral. The four-region arrangement is approximately tetrahedral. Now hide the lone pairs. What's left is an oxygen and two hydrogens, joined at an angle. Water is bent. And the angle? About one hundred and four point five degrees. Smaller again. Two lone pairs crowd the bonds more than one did, so the angle closes a little further. Put the three side by side. Methane, no lone pairs, one hundred and nine point five. Ammonia, one lone pair, about one hundred and seven. Water, two lone pairs, about one hundred and four point five. Same four regions, same tetrahedral electron geometry, three different molecular shapes. A word of honesty, though. VSEPR is a qualitative model. For methane, ammonia and water, it explains the decreasing angles. The exact numbers are measured, and they depend on the atoms involved and on the full electronic structure. There is no simple formula that turns lone pairs into degrees. Now look back at our opening mystery. Water's oxygen has four regions, two of them invisible lone pairs. Carbon dioxide's carbon has just two regions, two double bonds, and no lone pairs. Water has four regions, but only two bonded atoms. Carbon has two regions. Their different electron arrangements solve our mystery. And the same counting works for bigger molecules too.
