The same test works in three dimensions. Boron trifluoride: three polar bonds, pointing out to the fluorines. Chain them tip to tail. The path closes into a triangle, and returns to where it started. Total: zero. Methane: four small bond dipoles, pointing in toward carbon, toward the centre of a tetrahedron. Chain them in three dimensions, and again the path closes. Methane is nonpolar. Ammonia: three bonds pulling toward nitrogen, arranged as a pyramid. This time the chain doesn't close. All three bond arrows share an upward lean. The full electron distribution sets the actual dipole. Ammonia is polar, and its overall dipole points toward the nitrogen, on the lone pair side. Line them up. Carbon dioxide, boron trifluoride and methane are symmetric, and their bond dipoles cancel. Nonpolar. Ammonia and water are lopsided, and their dipoles add up. Polar. To be fair, shape is not the only factor. The atoms matter, and so does how strongly each bond is polarised. For these molecules, symmetry tells us whether the equivalent bond dipoles cancel. Counting regions gives us the arrangement to test.
