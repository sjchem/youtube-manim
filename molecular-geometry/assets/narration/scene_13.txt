Carbon's valence orbitals are one two s orbital, a sphere, and three two p orbitals, each a pair of lobes along one axis. The three p orbitals sit at right angles, ninety degrees apart. But methane's bonds are one hundred and nine point five degrees apart. The atomic orbitals don't line up with them. Here's the trick. In quantum mechanics, orbitals are wave functions, and wave functions can be added. Mix some s with some p, and you get a new function: a hybrid orbital. Turn this dial. All s, and it's a sphere. Add some p, and one side grows while the other shrinks. All p, and it's the familiar pair of lobes. In between, the orbital points in one direction. This is a mathematical model, not orbitals physically melting together. We're choosing a convenient way to describe the same electrons. Mix one s with one p, half and half, and you get two S P hybrids. They point in opposite directions, one hundred and eighty degrees apart, like the two regions of carbon dioxide.
