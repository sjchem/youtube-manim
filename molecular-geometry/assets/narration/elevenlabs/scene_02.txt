[warm, conversational delivery] Last time, we discovered why atoms bond at all. Sharing electrons lowers the energy, and overlapping orbitals make sigma and pi bonds. We drew those bonds with Lewis structures: letters for atoms, lines for shared pairs, dots for lone pairs. Here is methane. One carbon, four hydrogens, four single bonds, drawn as a neat flat cross. A Lewis structure is excellent bookkeeping. It tells us which atoms are connected, and where the electron pairs are counted. [curious] But it is drawn on flat paper. So is methane really flat, with its bonds ninety degrees apart? Turn the flat version on its side, and it collapses into a line. Every bond is squeezed into one plane. [warmly] The real molecule does something smarter. The four bonds lift out of the plane and spread through all three dimensions. They point to the corners of a tetrahedron, about one hundred and nine point five degrees apart. Wider than ninety. So the Lewis structure tells us the connections. It doesn't tell us the shape. [short pause] [thoughtful] To predict shape, we need to think about the space that electrons occupy around an atom.
