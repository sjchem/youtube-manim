## Description

A car holds a steady 20 metres per second on a straight road: no
acceleration. Put it on a circular track at exactly the same speed, and it
accelerates the whole time. How?

Episode 02 of Visualized Physics — From Motion to the Quantum World follows
one idea from Episode 01: velocity is a vector, and direction matters. See
why the velocity in circular motion is always tangent, why the change in
velocity points to the centre, and derive a = v²/r twice — from the velocity
triangle and from the velocity circle. Watch Newton's coast-and-nudge polygon
become a circle, measure motion with radians, angular velocity, period and
frequency, and meet the forces that do the inward job: tension (and what
happens when the string snaps), friction on a flat curve (v_max = √(μ_s g r)),
and gravity (Newton's cannon, v = √(GM/r), a 400 km orbit in about 92
minutes). Then centrifugal force, seen from two frames at once, and
nonuniform circular motion with its radial and tangential parts. Five
conceptual challenges at the end.

One particle carries the mathematics throughout: 2 kg on a 2 m circle at
4 m/s — 2 rad/s, a period of π seconds, 0.318 Hz, 8 m/s², 16 N.

Assumptions: uniform circular motion unless stated; g = 9.8 m/s²; the curve
is level and idealized (not a safe-driving guide); ideal circular orbits
around a spherically symmetric Earth.

Sources: OpenStax University Physics Volume 1 (§4.4, §6.3, §10.1–10.3, §13.4);
NASA Earth Fact Sheet.
