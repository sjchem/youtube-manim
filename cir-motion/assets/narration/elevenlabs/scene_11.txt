[warm, conversational delivery] [curious] If the acceleration points toward the centre, why doesn't the object simply move into the centre? Because acceleration doesn't tell us which way an object is moving. Velocity does. Acceleration tells us how that velocity is changing. Let's build the motion step by step, the way Newton did. Let the object coast in a straight line for a moment. Then give it a brief nudge toward the centre. Its velocity turns a little, and it coasts again. [short pause] Coast, nudge, coast, nudge. The corners of the polygon stay on the same circle. Make the nudges smaller and more frequent, and the polygon becomes a circle. The inward pull never draws the object in. It only bends its path. In vector form, the acceleration is minus omega squared times the position vector r. Or minus v squared over r, times r hat. The minus sign means inward, opposite to the outward unit vector r hat. One more subtlety. The magnitude of the acceleration is constant, but its direction isn't. It always points to the centre from wherever the object is. So the acceleration vector itself keeps turning. [pause] [warmly] Circular motion isn't an exception to the laws of motion. It's what a changing velocity looks like, when the change is always sideways.
