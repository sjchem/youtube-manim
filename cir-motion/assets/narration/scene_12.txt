Now that we know why circular motion needs acceleration, we need a language for describing motion around a circle. On a straight line, we measure distance. On a circle, a more natural measure is the angle the radius has swept out from a fixed direction. Measure it in radians. Lay the radius itself along the circle. The angle that holds exactly one radius of arc is one radian. In general, the angle in radians is the arc length divided by the radius: theta equals s over r. Or, s equals r theta. A full circle has a circumference of two pi r, so a full turn is two pi radians. Three hundred and sixty degrees is two pi radians. Half a turn, one hundred and eighty degrees, is pi radians. A quarter turn, ninety degrees, is pi over two. Radians tie an angle directly to a distance along the circle. That's what makes s equals r theta so simple. Angular position is the angle at one instant. Angular displacement is the change: delta theta equals theta final minus theta initial. By convention, counterclockwise is positive, and clockwise is negative. And if we keep counting, one full counterclockwise lap is an angular displacement of two pi, even though the object ends up facing the same way it started.
