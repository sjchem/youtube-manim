If the velocity arrows all have the same length, how can their difference produce an acceleration? And which way does it point? Take the object at one instant, with velocity v one. A moment later it's here, with velocity v two. The lengths are equal: both are v. Only the direction is different. To find the change, slide v two back so the two arrows share a tail. Delta v runs from the tip of v one to the tip of v two. Average acceleration is delta v divided by delta t, so it points the same way as delta v. Now carry delta v back to the circle. It points inward, toward the centre, as seen from the middle of the arc between the two moments. Shrink the time interval. The two moments close in on one instant, and delta v swings until it points straight at the centre. In the limit, the instantaneous acceleration points exactly inward. It's called centripetal acceleration. Centripetal means centre-seeking. That word describes a direction, not a cause. We'll come to the cause later. So velocity is along the tangent, and acceleration points to the centre. They're perpendicular: v dot a is zero. A perpendicular acceleration turns the velocity without changing its length. That's uniform circular motion.
