## Scene 01 — Can you accelerate without speeding up? — 00:00–01:22

Imagine a car on a perfectly straight road, holding a steady twenty metres per second. It isn't speeding up. It isn't slowing down. And it isn't changing direction. As far as physics is concerned, its acceleration is zero. Now put the same car on a circular track, still at exactly twenty metres per second. Nothing has changed on the speedometer. But watch the arrow of its velocity. It never stops turning. In physics, changing direction means changing velocity. And a changing velocity means acceleration. So here's the surprising conclusion. This car is accelerating the whole time, even though it never gets any faster. Why does that happen? Which way does the acceleration point? And what force makes it possible? To find out, we'll follow one idea from Episode one: velocity is a vector, and direction matters. This is Circular Motion.

## Scene 02 — What is acceleration? — 01:22–02:36

In everyday language, accelerating means speeding up. A car going from twenty to sixty kilometres per hour is accelerating. Physics uses a more precise definition. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: a equals d v d t. And velocity is a vector. It has a magnitude, the speed, and a direction. So velocity can change in three ways. Its magnitude can change. Its direction can change. Or both can change at once. Every one of these is acceleration. On the straight road, neither the speed nor the direction changed, so the acceleration was zero. On the circular track, the speed stayed fixed, but the direction kept changing, so the acceleration was not zero. That's the key idea of this episode. Constant speed does not mean constant velocity. Zero acceleration needs constant velocity; constant speed alone isn't enough.

## Scene 03 — Speed vs velocity — 02:36–04:08

Let's make that precise, using the vector tools from Episode one. Speed is a scalar. Velocity is a vector. And speed is simply the magnitude of the velocity: v equals the length of the vector v. Take two velocities. The first is five metres per second along the x axis: five i hat. The second is five metres per second along the y axis: five j hat. Both have a speed of five metres per second. But they are not the same vector. How much has the velocity changed? Subtract: delta v equals v two minus v one. Reverse the first arrow and add it tip to tail. Delta v is minus five i hat plus five j hat. Its length, by Pythagoras, is the square root of twenty-five plus twenty-five: about seven point zero seven metres per second. The speed didn't change at all. Yet the velocity changed by more than seven metres per second, just by turning. A change of direction alone can produce a real change in velocity. And that is exactly what happens, continuously, in circular motion.

## Scene 04 — Which way is it moving? — 04:08–05:16

Picture an object moving around a circle of radius r. At every instant, it has a definite velocity. But which way does that velocity point? Draw the position vector, r, from the centre of the circle to the object. The velocity points along the tangent: the line that just touches the circle at that point. And the tangent is always perpendicular to the radius. Velocity is perpendicular to r. So the object is never moving toward the centre, and never directly outward. At each moment, it's heading off along the tangent. As it moves on, the tangent turns, and the velocity turns with it. Same length, new direction, every instant. Let's see what that turning does to the components of the velocity.

## Scene 05 — Velocity in components — 05:16–06:46

Put the centre of the circle at the origin. The object's position is r cos theta i hat, plus r sin theta j hat. Here theta is the angle from the x axis. For uniform motion, it grows steadily: theta equals omega t, plus theta zero. Omega is the angular velocity, how fast the angle grows. We'll look at it closely later. Now add time as a third direction. The circle stretches out into a helix. Its shadow on one wall rises and falls as a cosine wave. On the other wall, as a sine wave. Take the time derivative, and the velocity is minus r omega sine theta i hat, plus r omega cosine theta j hat. Both components keep changing as the object moves round. But the magnitude doesn't. Square and add: r squared omega squared, times sine squared plus cosine squared, and that bracket is always one. So the speed is v equals r times the size of omega. Constant, while the vector itself keeps rotating.

## Scene 06 — One full lap — 06:46–08:06

Here's an idea from Episode one, revisited on a circle. Run once around a circular track of radius r. Your feet carry you the whole circumference: a distance of two pi r. Now watch the displacement: the arrow from where you started to where you are. It grows, swings round, and shrinks back to nothing. When you finish, you're standing exactly where you began. Zero displacement means zero average velocity over the lap: delta r over delta t is zero. Yet you were moving the whole time. Your average speed was two pi r over T, where T is the time for one lap. In uniform circular motion, that's also your constant speed. And zero average velocity says nothing about acceleration. At every instant during the lap, your velocity was turning. So instantaneous velocity points along the tangent, and in circular motion its direction never stops changing. Which brings us to the heart of this episode.

## Scene 07 — Where does delta v point? — 08:06–09:57

If the velocity arrows all have the same length, how can their difference produce an acceleration? And which way does it point? Take the object at one instant, with velocity v one. A moment later it's here, with velocity v two. The lengths are equal: both are v. Only the direction is different. To find the change, slide v two back so the two arrows share a tail. Delta v runs from the tip of v one to the tip of v two. Average acceleration is delta v divided by delta t, so it points the same way as delta v. Now carry delta v back to the circle. It points inward, toward the centre, as seen from the middle of the arc between the two moments. Shrink the time interval. The two moments close in on one instant, and delta v swings until it points straight at the centre. In the limit, the instantaneous acceleration points exactly inward. It's called centripetal acceleration. Centripetal means centre-seeking. That word describes a direction, not a cause. We'll come to the cause later. So velocity is along the tangent, and acceleration points to the centre. They're perpendicular: v dot a is zero. A perpendicular acceleration turns the velocity without changing its length. That's uniform circular motion.

## Scene 08 — The velocity triangle — 09:57–11:34

How big is that acceleration? Let's derive it, rather than just state it. In a short time delta t, the object moves through a small angle, delta theta. The velocity is always perpendicular to the radius, so it turns through the same angle: delta theta. Here's a lovely way to see it. Collect every velocity arrow at one shared tail. Their tips trace out a circle of their own, with radius v. As the object goes round its circle once, the velocity arrow goes round this velocity circle once, in step. Two velocity arrows and delta v make an isosceles triangle: two sides of length v, with the angle delta theta between them. Split it down the middle. Each half is a right triangle, so delta v is two v sine of half delta theta. For small angles in radians, two sine of half the angle is almost exactly the angle itself. Watch the ratio as the angle shrinks: ninety degrees, forty-five, twenty, five. It closes in on one. So for a short interval, the size of delta v is v times delta theta.

## Scene 09 — Deriving v squared over r — 11:34–13:25

Now divide by delta t to get the average acceleration: v times delta theta over delta t. We still need delta theta over delta t. Look back at the path. An arc of angle delta theta has length r times delta theta. At constant speed, that arc length is also v times delta t. So r delta theta equals v delta t, and delta theta over delta t is v over r. Substitute: the acceleration is v times v over r. Take the limit of a vanishing interval, and the approximation becomes exact. The centripetal acceleration is v squared over r, pointing toward the centre. There's a second way to see the same result. The position arrow sweeps round a circle of radius r, and its tip moves at speed v. The velocity arrow sweeps round a circle of radius v, at the same angular rate, v over r. So its tip moves at v times v over r. And the speed of the velocity's tip is the acceleration. Let's put in numbers. A particle moves at four metres per second around a circle of radius two metres. Four squared is sixteen. Sixteen over two: a centripetal acceleration of eight metres per second squared. And the particle's speed never leaves four metres per second. We'll keep this particle with us for the rest of the episode.

## Scene 10 — Speed and radius — 13:25–14:30

The formula tells us something that isn't obvious at all. Speed enters squared. Double the speed on the same circle, and the acceleration doesn't double. It becomes four times larger. For our particle, moving at eight metres per second instead of four would take thirty-two metres per second squared instead of eight. The radius sits underneath. Keep the speed fixed and double the radius, and the acceleration halves, to four metres per second squared. Why? On a wider circle, the object takes longer to turn through the same angle, so its velocity changes direction more gently. That's why the same speed can feel easy on a broad curve, but demanding on a sharp bend. Tight turns at high speed need the most acceleration of all.

## Scene 11 — Why doesn't it fall inward? — 14:30–16:17

If the acceleration points toward the centre, why doesn't the object simply move into the centre? Because acceleration doesn't tell us which way an object is moving. Velocity does. Acceleration tells us how that velocity is changing. Let's build the motion step by step, the way Newton did. Let the object coast in a straight line for a moment. Then give it a brief nudge toward the centre. Its velocity turns a little, and it coasts again. Coast, nudge, coast, nudge. The corners of the polygon stay on the same circle. Make the nudges smaller and more frequent, and the polygon becomes a circle. The inward pull never draws the object in. It only bends its path. In vector form, the acceleration is minus omega squared times the position vector r. Or minus v squared over r, times r hat. The minus sign means inward, opposite to the outward unit vector r hat. One more subtlety. The magnitude of the acceleration is constant, but its direction isn't. It always points to the centre from wherever the object is. So the acceleration vector itself keeps turning. Circular motion isn't an exception to the laws of motion. It's what a changing velocity looks like, when the change is always sideways.

## Scene 12 — Angles and radians — 16:17–18:05

Now that we know why circular motion needs acceleration, we need a language for describing motion around a circle. On a straight line, we measure distance. On a circle, a more natural measure is the angle the radius has swept out from a fixed direction. Measure it in radians. Lay the radius itself along the circle. The angle that holds exactly one radius of arc is one radian. In general, the angle in radians is the arc length divided by the radius: theta equals s over r. Or, s equals r theta. A full circle has a circumference of two pi r, so a full turn is two pi radians. Three hundred and sixty degrees is two pi radians. Half a turn, one hundred and eighty degrees, is pi radians. A quarter turn, ninety degrees, is pi over two. Radians tie an angle directly to a distance along the circle. That's what makes s equals r theta so simple. Angular position is the angle at one instant. Angular displacement is the change: delta theta equals theta final minus theta initial. By convention, counterclockwise is positive, and clockwise is negative. And if we keep counting, one full counterclockwise lap is an angular displacement of two pi, even though the object ends up facing the same way it started.

## Scene 13 — Angular velocity — 18:05–19:56

How quickly does an object move around a circle? Angular velocity answers that. Linear velocity is the rate of change of position. Angular velocity, omega, is the rate of change of angle: omega equals d theta d t. It's measured in radians per second. Counterclockwise counts as positive, clockwise as negative. For uniform circular motion, omega is constant, so the angle grows linearly: theta of t equals theta zero plus omega t. Now connect it to ordinary speed. Start with s equals r theta, and differentiate with respect to time. With a fixed radius, d s d t is r times d theta d t. The speed is v equals r times the size of omega. Here's what that means. Watch a spinning turntable, with one marker near the centre and one near the edge. Both complete each turn together. They share the same angular velocity. But in one turn, the outer marker travels a longer path. So it moves faster: its linear speed is greater. At two radians per second, a point one metre from the centre moves at two metres per second. A point two metres out moves at four. That outer point is our reference particle. Its angular speed is v over r, four over two: two radians per second. It sweeps out two radians every second.

## Scene 14 — Period and frequency — 19:56–21:30

Circular motion repeats. So we can ask two more questions about it. How long does one revolution take? That's the period, T, measured in seconds. And how many revolutions happen each second? That's the frequency, f, measured in hertz. One hertz is one cycle per second. They're reciprocals: f equals one over T, and T equals one over f. A shorter period means more turns every second. One revolution sweeps two pi radians in a time T. So the angular speed is two pi over T, which is two pi f. And one revolution covers two pi r of distance, so the speed is v equals two pi r over T. Back to our particle. Its period is two pi r over v: two pi times two, over four. Exactly pi seconds, about three point one four seconds. Its frequency is one over pi: about zero point three one eight hertz. And two pi times that frequency gives back two radians per second, just as before. Angular speed, period, frequency and linear speed aren't four separate facts. They're four ways of describing one motion.

## Scene 15 — Angular acceleration — 21:30–22:28

There's one more angular quantity. What if the angular velocity itself starts to change? Angular acceleration, alpha, is the rate of change of angular velocity: alpha equals d omega d t, in radians per second squared. In uniform circular motion, omega is constant, so alpha is zero. But that doesn't mean the acceleration is zero. Our particle still has a centripetal acceleration of eight metres per second squared. The two measure different things. Angular acceleration tracks how the turning rate changes. Centripetal acceleration is the inward acceleration that keeps changing the velocity's direction. Keep that distinction in mind. It will matter when the object starts to speed up.

## Scene 16 — Centripetal force — 22:28–23:50

Circular motion requires an inward acceleration. But acceleration doesn't happen by itself. What causes it? Newton's second law: the net force equals mass times acceleration. In uniform circular motion, the acceleration points toward the centre. So the net force must point toward the centre too. That required inward net force is called the centripetal force. Its size is m v squared over r, or, with angular speed, m r omega squared. For our particle, with a mass of two kilograms: two times four squared, over two. A net inward force of sixteen newtons. Now the most important point. Centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It's a name for a job: the net inward force needed to keep an object on a circle. Something real must do that job. For a ball on a string, it's tension. For a car on a flat road, friction. For a satellite, gravity. Different forces. The same requirement. Let's look at each one.

## Scene 17 — A ball on a string — 23:50–25:23

Imagine a small ball on a smooth horizontal table, tied to a string and moving in a circle. The string stays taut, and friction is negligible. The ball always has a tangential velocity. But the string pulls it inward, toward the centre. That tension is the only horizontal force, so the tension provides the centripetal force: T equals m v squared over r. Now, suppose the string suddenly snaps. Where does the ball go? Many people expect it to fly straight outward, away from the centre. It doesn't. The moment the string breaks, the inward force disappears. With no horizontal force left, the ball keeps the velocity it had at that instant. And that velocity was along the tangent. So the ball moves off in a straight line, along the tangent. That's Newton's first law. Without a net force, velocity stays constant. The ball never needed a force to keep moving. It needed a force to keep turning. On a vertical circle, gravity would still act after release, so the ball would follow a projectile's curve instead.

## Scene 18 — Why cars need friction — 25:23–27:34

Now a car taking a curve on a flat, level road, at constant speed. Its direction is changing, so it needs a centripetal acceleration, and therefore an inward force. On a flat road, that force comes from static friction between the tyres and the road. On level ground, the normal force balances the weight: N equals m g. Static friction can grow only up to mu s times N, which is mu s m g. To follow the curve without sliding, the force needed can't exceed the friction available: m v squared over r must be at most mu s m g. The mass cancels. So v squared over r is at most mu s g, and the fastest possible speed is the square root of mu s g r. Take a curve of radius fifty metres, with a friction coefficient of zero point six. The square root of zero point six, times nine point eight, times fifty: about seventeen point one five metres per second, roughly sixty-one point seven kilometres per hour. At fifteen metres per second, the car needs four point five metres per second squared. Friction can supply up to five point eight eight. It holds the curve. At twenty metres per second, it would need eight. Friction can't supply that much, so the tyres slide and the car drifts wide. Because the demand grows with the square of speed, a small increase in speed can ask a lot more of the tyres. This is an idealized model. Real handling depends on much more, so it isn't a guide to a safe driving speed.

## Scene 19 — Why satellites orbit — 27:34–29:38

A satellite circles Earth with no string to pull it and no road to grip. So what keeps it on its path? Gravity. Picture a cannon on a very tall mountain, firing horizontally. The faster the shot, the farther it travels before it lands. Fire it fast enough, and the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls. It never lands. So an orbiting satellite is falling the whole time. Its sideways speed keeps carrying it past the edge of the Earth. For a circular orbit, gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force. G M m over r squared equals m v squared over r. The satellite's mass cancels. G M over r equals v squared, so the orbital speed is the square root of G M over r. A small satellite and a huge space station, at the same radius, need the same orbital speed. Take a circular orbit four hundred kilometres above Earth's surface. The orbital speed is about seven point six seven kilometres per second. Gravity up there is still about eight point seven metres per second squared. Not gone at all. Satellites orbit because gravity acts on them. One lap of two pi r at that speed gives the period: T equals two pi times the square root of r cubed over G M. Here, about ninety-two minutes. That holds for an ideal circular orbit around a spherically symmetric Earth. And it rests on the same idea as a ball on a string.

## Scene 20 — Is there a centrifugal force? — 29:38–31:12

When a car turns sharply, you feel pushed outward, toward the door. If the force is inward, what is that push? Watch from above, from the ground, which isn't rotating. Your body tends to keep moving in a straight line. That's inertia. The car turns underneath you, until the door or the seat pushes on you. That push points inward. It's the force that makes you turn with the car. In this frame, there's no outward force at all. Only a real inward one. Now ride along inside the car, in a frame that rotates with it. From here, you seem to be thrown outward. In the rotating frame, the outward centrifugal term is m omega squared r. While you slide, a Coriolis term is needed too. These are inertial forces, needed because the frame rotates. They are not extra physical pushes. So centripetal force is the real net inward force, seen from a non-rotating frame. Centrifugal force belongs to the rotating frame. Circular motion needs a net inward force. Tension, friction and gravity are different ways to provide it.

## Scene 21 — Uniform circular motion — 31:12–31:48

So far, we've studied circular motion at a constant speed. That's what we call uniform circular motion. The speed is constant. On a fixed circle, so is the angular speed. The angular acceleration is zero. And the acceleration is entirely radial: v squared over r, pointing inward. There's no component along the tangent. That inward acceleration redirects the velocity, while preserving its magnitude. But what if the object also speeds up?

## Scene 22 — Speeding up on a circle — 31:48–33:39

Now imagine the object travels around the circle while gradually going faster. Its direction is still changing, so it still needs a radial acceleration toward the centre: a r equals v squared over r. But its speed is changing too, and that needs a second component, along the tangent: a t equals d v d t. Speeding up, the tangential part points along the velocity. Slowing down, it points against it. On a fixed circle, the tangential acceleration is r times alpha, the angular acceleration. In full: a equals minus r omega squared r hat, plus r alpha theta hat. Here r hat points outward, and theta hat along the positive tangent. The two parts are perpendicular, so the total magnitude follows Pythagoras: the square root of a r squared plus a t squared. Our particle again. At four metres per second, on its two metre circle, the radial part is eight metres per second squared. Now give it an angular acceleration of zero point five radians per second squared. The tangential part is two times zero point five: one metre per second squared. Altogether, the square root of sixty-four plus one: about eight point zero six metres per second squared. The total arrow tips slightly forward of the centre. One part turns the velocity. The other stretches it.

## Scene 23 — Speed, direction, or both? — 33:39–34:24

Let's put every case side by side. Straight line, constant velocity: no tangential acceleration, no radial acceleration. Nothing changes. Straight line, changing speed: tangential acceleration only. Uniform circular motion: radial acceleration only. Circular motion with changing speed: both. So acceleration isn't only about getting faster. It can change the speed, the direction, or both at once. These aren't different definitions. They're all one principle: acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

## Scene 25 — One chain of reasoning — 36:14–37:30

We began with a question. Can an object accelerate without ever getting faster? The answer is yes. Acceleration isn't about speed alone. It's about changing velocity. And because velocity is a vector, changing direction is enough. Changing direction means changing velocity, which means acceleration. By Newton's second law, acceleration requires a net force. For uniform circular motion, both point toward the centre. Our one particle tied it all together: four metres per second, two radians per second, a period of pi seconds, eight metres per second squared, sixteen newtons. One motion, many descriptions. A ball on a string, a car on a curve, a satellite in orbit. Different forces, the same reasoning. Motion isn't determined by speed alone. Direction matters just as much. Once we understand how velocity changes, we can understand why objects follow the paths they do. That's the power of thinking in vectors.
