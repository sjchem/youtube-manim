Chapter 1 — Introduction: Can We Predict Motion?
00:00–02:30

TOPIC: Understanding motion as a physical phenomenon that we can describe and predict mathematically.

MAIN QUESTION: If you throw a ball into the air, can physics tell you precisely where it will be at a future moment?
Core science
Think about what you would need to know to describe a moving object completely. Its position changes with time, but that alone isn't the whole story.

Three fundamental questions help us understand its motion:
- Where is the object?
- How fast and in what direction is it moving?
- How is its velocity changing?
These lead us to three essential physical quantities: position, velocity, and acceleration.

An important point to establish immediately is that speed and direction are different kinds of information.

Imagine two objects moving at 10 m/s. One moves east, and the other moves north. Their speeds are identical, but their velocities are not.

Now consider a ball flying through the air. Its direction changes throughout the flight. So, knowing its speed alone won't allow us to predict its trajectory.
Central scientific idea
We can understand a complicated two-dimensional motion by separating it into two coordinate directions and applying the appropriate equations to each.

This principle will eventually connect every major topic in the episode.
Narration idea
Imagine throwing a ball into the air. You already know what will happen: it will rise, continue moving forward, and eventually return to the ground.

But can we predict exactly where the ball will be after one second? What determines its maximum height? And why does changing the angle of the throw change how far it travels?

At first, the curved trajectory seems like a complicated problem.

Yet physics gives us a remarkably simple way to understand it.

Instead of treating the entire motion as one complicated event, we can separate it into simpler parts.

To do that, we first need to understand the mathematical language of motion: vectors.

Key takeaway: If we want to predict motion accurately, numbers alone aren't always enough. Direction matters too.

Chapter 2 — Scalars and Vectors
02:30–07:25
Topic 2.1 — What Is a Scalar?
MAIN QUESTION: Can every physical quantity be described completely using just a number and a unit?
Core science
Start by introducing the simplest type of physical quantity: the scalar.

A scalar is described by a numerical value and an appropriate unit, without a spatial direction. Its value can be signed; magnitude means absolute size and is never negative. Some scalars, such as ratios, are dimensionless.

Examples:
Scalar quantity	Example
Mass	5 kg
Time	10 s
Temperature	25°C
Distance	100 m
Speed	20 m/s
Energy	50 J

A useful distinction is that scalars aren't necessarily positive. Depending on the quantity and how it's defined, a scalar may be positive, negative, or zero.

For instance, −5°C is still a scalar temperature. The minus sign doesn't represent a direction in physical space.
Math idea
Speed describes how quickly distance is traveled.

Average speed is defined as:
\[
\text{Average speed}=\frac{\text{Total distance}}{\text{Elapsed time}}
\]

Suppose an object travels 100 meters in 10 seconds.

Its average speed is:
\[
\frac{100}{10}=10\text{ m/s}
\]

We now know how quickly it covered that distance. But we still don't know which direction it traveled.

That missing information leads directly to vectors.
Narration idea
Think about the speedometer in a car.

It might tell you that the car is moving at 20 meters per second. But from that measurement alone, you can't tell whether the car is traveling north, south, east, or west.

For some physical quantities, a numerical value and an appropriate unit are enough.

But to describe motion through space, we often need one more piece of information: direction.


Topic 2.2 — What Is a Vector?
MAIN QUESTION: What makes vectors different from ordinary numerical quantities?
Core science
A vector obeys the rules of vector addition. A nonzero vector has a magnitude and a definite direction; the zero vector has magnitude zero and no uniquely defined direction.

Important examples include:
- Displacement
- Velocity
- Acceleration
- Force
- Momentum
Introduce the standard mathematical notation:
\[
\vec A
\]

This represents a vector.

Its magnitude is written as:
\[
|\vec A|
\]

In two-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, we can describe the vector as:
\[
\vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j
\]

Here:
- \(A_x\) represents the horizontal component.
- \(A_y\) represents the vertical component.
- \(\hat i\) is the unit vector along the x-axis.
- \(\hat j\) is the unit vector along the y-axis.
You don't need to fully explain the component equations yet. At this point, the purpose is simply to introduce the idea that a vector can describe movement in more than one direction.
Narration idea
A vector gives us two pieces of information at once.

Its magnitude tells us how much of a quantity we're dealing with, while its direction tells us where that quantity points.

If I tell you that a car is moving at 20 meters per second, I've described its speed.

If I tell you it's moving eastward at 20 meters per second, I've described its velocity.

That difference may seem small, but it changes the mathematics of motion completely.


Topic 2.3 — Distance vs. Displacement
MAIN QUESTION: Can you travel a significant distance and still have zero displacement?
Core science
This is an opportunity to show why the distinction between scalars and vectors is physically meaningful.

Distance measures the total length of the path traveled.

Displacement describes the change between initial and final positions.

Mathematically:
\[
\Delta\vec r=\vec r_f-\vec r_i
\]

Consider someone walking 3 meters east and then 4 meters north.

Total distance:
\[
d=3+4=7\text{ m}
\]

Magnitude of displacement:
\[
|\Delta\vec r|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\text{ m}
\]

Direction:
\[
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac43\right)\approx53.1^\circ
\]

So, even though the person has traveled 7 meters, their final displacement is 5 meters, approximately 53.1° north of east.

Now take that idea further.

Suppose someone completes one lap around a circular running track and returns to the starting point.

They have traveled a positive distance. However, because their final position equals their initial position, their displacement is zero.
Narration idea
Distance keeps track of the entire path you've traveled.

Displacement asks a different question: where did you finish compared with where you started?

You could walk hundreds of meters around a track and end up exactly where you began.

In that case, your distance is certainly not zero. But your displacement is.

And understanding that difference is essential before we introduce velocity.

Chapter takeaway: Scalars describe magnitude; vectors describe magnitude and direction. Distance and displacement measure different aspects of the same journey.

Chapter 3 — Vector Addition and Subtraction
07:25–13:20
Topic 3.1 — Why Do We Add Vectors?
MAIN QUESTION: When multiple movements occur, how can we determine their combined effect?
Core science
Vector addition allows us to combine different directional quantities into a single resultant.

For two vectors:
\[
\vec R=\vec A+\vec B
\]

The resultant describes the total effect of those vectors.

But vector addition isn't always the same as adding their magnitudes.

Consider two examples.

If you move 5 meters east and then another 5 meters east, your displacement is 10 meters east.

If you move 5 meters east and then 5 meters west, your final displacement is zero.

The distances traveled may be equal in both cases, but their resultant displacement vectors are very different.

The reason is direction.
Mathematical properties
Vector addition is commutative:
\[
\vec A+\vec B=\vec B+\vec A
\]

And associative:
\[
(\vec A+\vec B)+\vec C
=\vec A+(\vec B+\vec C)
\]

These properties allow us to combine several vectors without changing the final result by changing the order or grouping.
Narration idea
When we add vectors, we're combining their effects.

But unlike ordinary numbers, vectors can point in different directions.

Two vectors might reinforce each other. They might partially cancel. Or, if they have equal magnitudes and opposite directions, they can cancel completely.

So we can't always add their magnitudes and expect the correct answer.

We have to account for where each vector points.


Topic 3.2 — Triangle and Parallelogram Laws
MAIN QUESTION: How can geometry help us calculate the resultant of two vectors?
Core science
Introduce the two standard geometric methods.

Triangle law: Place the tail of the second vector at the head of the first. The resultant runs from the starting point of the first vector to the endpoint of the second.

Parallelogram law: Place the two vectors at the same starting point. The diagonal of the parallelogram formed by those vectors represents their resultant.

The two approaches are mathematically equivalent.

If vectors have magnitudes \(A\) and \(B\), with an angle \(\phi\) between them, the resultant magnitude is:
\[
\boxed{R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\phi}}
\]

Now consider the particularly important case of perpendicular vectors.

When:
\[
\phi=90^\circ
\]

And since:
\[
\cos90^\circ=0
\]

The formula simplifies to:
\[
R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2}
\]

This is the Pythagorean theorem expressed as a vector calculation.
Worked example
Suppose an object moves 6 meters east and then 8 meters north.
\[
R=\sqrt{6^2+8^2}
=\sqrt{100}
=10\text{ m}
\]

The direction is:
\[
\theta=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac86\right)
\approx53.13^\circ
\]

The resultant displacement is 10 meters at approximately 53.13° north of east.
Narration idea
Imagine making two separate movements, each in a different direction.

Is there a single movement that would take you from the same starting point to the same final position?

Yes. That's the resultant displacement.

Geometry allows us to calculate both its magnitude and direction.

And when the movements are perpendicular, the familiar Pythagorean theorem becomes a powerful tool for vector addition.


Topic 3.3 — Vector Subtraction
MAIN QUESTION: What does subtracting one vector from another actually mean?
Core science
Vector subtraction is closely related to vector addition.

Subtracting a vector is equivalent to adding its negative:
\[
\vec A-\vec B=\vec A+(-\vec B)
\]

The negative of a vector has the same magnitude but points in the opposite direction.

For example:
\[
\vec A=(5,3)
\]

\[
\vec B=(2,1)
\]

Then:
\[
\vec A-\vec B=(5-2,\;3-1)
\]

\[
\boxed{\vec A-\vec B=(3,2)}
\]

This operation becomes especially important when we calculate changes in physical quantities.

Consider velocity.

The change in velocity is:
\[
\Delta\vec v=\vec v_f-\vec v_i
\]

Because velocity is a vector, this change can result from a change in speed, direction, or both.

That gives you an early connection to acceleration—and sets up an important idea for the next episode on circular motion.
Narration idea
Subtracting vectors lets us compare two directional quantities.

In motion, one of the most useful comparisons is between the initial and final velocities of an object.

If those velocities are different, the object has experienced a change in velocity.

And here's something important: the velocities can be different even when their magnitudes are the same.

Simply changing direction can produce a change in velocity.

Chapter takeaway: Addition tells us the combined effect of vectors. Subtraction tells us how one vector differs from another.

Chapter 4 — Scalar Multiplication and Unit Vectors
13:20–16:20
Topic 4.1 — Multiplying a Vector by a Scalar
MAIN QUESTION: What happens to a vector when you multiply it by a number?
Core science
Scalar multiplication changes the magnitude of a vector and, depending on the sign of the scalar, may reverse its direction.
\[
\vec B=k\vec A
\]

Where \(k\) is a scalar.

There are three useful cases:
1. If \(k>1\), the vector becomes longer while preserving its direction.
2. If \(0<k<1\), the vector becomes shorter while preserving its direction.
3. If \(k<0\), the vector reverses direction, and its magnitude is multiplied by \(|k|\).
If \(k=0\), the result is the zero vector, which has no uniquely defined direction.
Example
Consider:
\[
\vec A=3\hat i+4\hat j
\]

Multiplying by 2 gives:
\[
2\vec A=6\hat i+8\hat j
\]

Multiplying by −1 gives:
\[
-\vec A=-3\hat i-4\hat j
\]

The first operation doubles the magnitude.

The second reverses the direction without changing the magnitude.
Narration idea
Multiplying a vector by a positive number changes how large it is without changing where it points.

Multiplying by a negative number reverses its direction.

This may seem like a simple mathematical rule, but it appears throughout physics.

It helps us connect different vector quantities through equations.

For instance, Newton's second law expresses the relationship between mass, acceleration, and net force:
\[
\vec F_{\mathrm{net}}=m\vec a
\]

Because mass is a positive scalar, the net force points in the same direction as the acceleration.

Topic 4.2 — Unit Vectors
MAIN QUESTION: Can we describe a direction without specifying how large the vector is?
Core science
A unit vector has a magnitude of exactly one.

In two-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, the standard unit vectors are:
\[
\hat i=(1,0)
\]

\[
\hat j=(0,1)
\]

Any two-dimensional vector can be written as:
\[
\vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j
\]

For any nonzero vector, we can calculate the unit vector along its direction:
\[
\hat A=\frac{\vec A}{|\vec A|}
\]

For example:
\[
\vec A=3\hat i+4\hat j
\]

Its magnitude is:
\[
|\vec A|=5
\]

So its unit vector is:
\[
\hat A=\frac35\hat i+\frac45\hat j
\]

This provides a way to separate the magnitude and direction of a vector mathematically.
Narration idea
Sometimes, all we need to know is which way a quantity points.

That's what unit vectors help us describe.

They allow us to express direction independently of magnitude.

And once we know both the magnitude and the unit direction, we can construct the full vector again.

Chapter takeaway: Scalar multiplication changes vector magnitude and potentially direction, while unit vectors provide a precise mathematical description of direction.

Chapter 5 — Vector Components and Resolution
16:20–21:15

This chapter forms the direct mathematical bridge between vector operations and projectile motion. Give it particular attention because the rest of the episode depends on it.
Topic 5.1 — Why Do We Resolve Vectors?
MAIN QUESTION: How can a diagonal vector be expressed using horizontal and vertical quantities?
Core science
Vector resolution means decomposing a vector into components along chosen directions.

In Cartesian coordinates, we usually choose two perpendicular axes: x and y. Keep the axes fixed while comparing components.

A vector can then be expressed as the sum of its horizontal and vertical components:
\[
\vec A=\vec A_x+\vec A_y
\]

Or:
\[
\vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j
\]

An important clarification for the viewer is that resolving a vector doesn't physically split the original quantity into separate objects.

We're simply describing the same vector using two coordinate directions.

This makes calculations easier because we can work with components independently.
Narration idea
Suppose a vector points diagonally.

At first, we might think of it as a single quantity with one magnitude and one direction.

But there's another way to describe it.

We can determine how much of that vector points horizontally and how much points vertically.

The two components combine to give exactly the original vector.

This idea is called vector resolution, and it's one of the most useful mathematical techniques in physics.


Topic 5.2 — Deriving Horizontal and Vertical Components
MAIN QUESTION: How do sine and cosine help us calculate vector components?
Core mathematics
Consider a vector \(\vec A\) with magnitude \(A\), making an angle \(\theta\) with the positive horizontal axis.

The horizontal component is related to the cosine of the angle:
\[
\cos\theta=\frac{A_x}{A}
\]

Rearranging:
\[
\boxed{A_x=A\cos\theta}
\]

The vertical component is related to the sine:
\[
\sin\theta=\frac{A_y}{A}
\]

Therefore:
\[
\boxed{A_y=A\sin\theta}
\]

These equations give the correct signed components when the angle is measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis.
Worked example
Suppose a vector has magnitude 10 units and points at 30° above the positive horizontal axis.

Then:
\[
A_x=10\cos30^\circ\approx8.66
\]

\[
A_y=10\sin30^\circ=5
\]

So the vector can be written as:
\[
\vec A\approx8.66\hat i+5\hat j
\]

Important extension — Reconstructing the vector
If we know the components, we can work backward.

The magnitude is:
\[
A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2}
\]

The direction is:
\[
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(A_y,A_x)
\]

Using \(\operatorname{atan2}\) rather than a simple inverse tangent avoids confusion about which quadrant contains the vector.
Narration idea
Sine and cosine aren't just abstract trigonometric functions.

In vector physics, they tell us how strongly a vector points along each coordinate direction.

When the angle is measured from the horizontal, cosine gives the horizontal contribution, while sine gives the vertical contribution.

So if we know a vector's magnitude and angle, we can calculate both components.

And if we know both components, we can reconstruct the original vector.


Topic 5.3 — Adding Vectors Using Components
MAIN QUESTION: What happens when we need to combine multiple vectors pointing in different directions?
Core science and mathematics
The component method makes vector addition an algebraic calculation.

Suppose:
\[
\vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j
\]

And:
\[
\vec B=B_x\hat i+B_y\hat j
\]

Their resultant is:
\[
\vec R=\vec A+\vec B
\]

We add the horizontal components:
\[
R_x=A_x+B_x
\]

And the vertical components:
\[
R_y=A_y+B_y
\]

Then:
\[
\vec R=(A_x+B_x)\hat i+(A_y+B_y)\hat j
\]

Worked example
Let:
\[
\vec A=4\hat i+3\hat j
\]

\[
\vec B=-2\hat i+5\hat j
\]

Adding:
\[
\vec R=(4-2)\hat i+(3+5)\hat j
\]

\[
\vec R=2\hat i+8\hat j
\]

The magnitude of the resultant is:
\[
|\vec R|=\sqrt{2^2+8^2}=\sqrt{68}\approx8.25
\]

Once the components are known, even vectors pointing in different directions can be combined systematically.
Narration idea
We now have a much more efficient way to add vectors.

Instead of solving the entire geometric problem at once, we work one coordinate direction at a time.

First, combine the horizontal components.

Then combine the vertical components.

Finally, those two results tell us the magnitude and direction of the resultant.

It's the same physical answer, but the mathematics becomes much more manageable.

Chapter takeaway: Resolving vectors into components allows us to handle complex directional calculations through simpler algebra. This is exactly the technique we'll use to study projectile motion.

Chapter 6 — Kinematics in One and Two Dimensions
21:15–27:15
Topic 6.1 — Position and Displacement
MAIN QUESTION: How can we mathematically describe where an object is located?
Core science
Before describing motion, we need a coordinate system and a reference point.

An object's position can be represented by a position vector.

In two dimensions:
\[
\vec r=x\hat i+y\hat j
\]

Here, x and y specify its coordinates relative to the chosen origin.

When an object moves between two positions, its displacement is:
\[
\Delta\vec r=\vec r_f-\vec r_i
\]

In component form:
\[
\Delta\vec r=(x_f-x_i)\hat i+(y_f-y_i)\hat j
\]

Unlike the coordinates of an individual position, displacement doesn't depend on where we choose the origin, provided we use the same coordinate system for both positions.
Narration idea
Before we can describe movement, we need to know where an object is.

A coordinate system gives us a precise way to define its position.

Once the object changes position, we can calculate its displacement.

But there's something displacement doesn't tell us by itself: how quickly the change took place.

To answer that, we introduce velocity.


Topic 6.2 — Velocity
MAIN QUESTION: How can we describe both the rate and direction of movement?
Core science
Average velocity is defined as displacement divided by elapsed time:
\[
\vec v_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta\vec r}{\Delta t}
\]

This tells us the average rate of change of position over a time interval.

Instantaneous velocity describes the rate of change at one specific instant:
\[
\vec v=\frac{d\vec r}{dt}
\]

In two dimensions, we write:
\[
\vec v=v_x\hat i+v_y\hat j
\]

Where:
\[
v_x=\frac{dx}{dt}
\]

\[
v_y=\frac{dy}{dt}
\]

The speed is the magnitude of the velocity vector:
\[
v=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}
\]

Important conceptual distinction
Speed tells us how fast an object is moving.

Velocity tells us both how fast it is moving and the direction of that movement.

Consequently, an object can maintain constant speed while its velocity changes because its direction is changing.
Narration idea
Velocity connects position and time.

It tells us how rapidly an object's position changes and in which direction.

And because velocity is a vector, its direction matters just as much as its magnitude.

An object can maintain exactly the same speed while its velocity continuously changes.

That's an important distinction we'll return to when we explore circular motion.


Topic 6.3 — Acceleration
MAIN QUESTION: Does acceleration always mean an object is speeding up?
Core science
Acceleration measures the rate at which velocity changes.

Average acceleration is:
\[
\vec a_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta\vec v}{\Delta t}
\]

Instantaneous acceleration is:
\[
\vec a=\frac{d\vec v}{dt}
\]

Since velocity is the derivative of position, acceleration is the second derivative of position:
\[
\vec a=\frac{d^2\vec r}{dt^2}
\]

Acceleration can occur when:
- Speed increases.
- Speed decreases.
- Direction changes.
- Both speed and direction change.
The key misconception to address is that acceleration always means becoming faster.

In physics, that's not the correct definition.
Narration idea
When we hear the word acceleration, we often imagine something speeding up.

But physics uses the word more precisely.

Acceleration means a change in velocity.

That change might involve speeding up, slowing down, or simply turning.

So even when an object's speed stays constant, its acceleration may not be zero.

Keep that idea in mind, because it will help us understand what happens at the highest point of a projectile's flight.


Topic 6.4 — Equations of Motion
MAIN QUESTION: If we know an object's initial velocity and acceleration, can we predict its future position?
Core mathematics
For motion with constant acceleration in one dimension, the fundamental equations are:
\[
\boxed{v=v_0+at}
\]

\[
\boxed{x=x_0+v_0t+\frac12at^2}
\]

And:
\[
\boxed{v^2=v_0^2+2a(x-x_0)}
\]

These equations apply when acceleration remains constant along the chosen axis.

Now extend the same reasoning into two dimensions.

Horizontal position:
\[
x(t)=x_0+v_{0x}t+\frac12a_xt^2
\]

Vertical position:
\[
y(t)=y_0+v_{0y}t+\frac12a_yt^2
\]

Horizontal velocity:
\[
v_x(t)=v_{0x}+a_xt
\]

Vertical velocity:
\[
v_y(t)=v_{0y}+a_yt
\]

There's no need to invent a completely new set of equations for motion in two dimensions.

We apply the same principles to each component.
Narration idea
These equations give us something remarkable: the ability to predict motion.

If we know where an object starts, how fast it's moving, and how it's accelerating, we can calculate its future position.

And for two-dimensional motion, the approach remains surprisingly simple.

We calculate the horizontal motion.

We calculate the vertical motion.

Then we combine the two to determine the full position of the object.

With that foundation, we're ready to return to our original question: how does a flying ball move through the air?

Chapter takeaway: Position, velocity, and acceleration are mathematically connected. For constant acceleration, their relationships allow us to predict motion in both one and two dimensions.

Chapter 7 — Projectile Motion
27:15–34:27
Topic 7.1 — What Is Projectile Motion?
MAIN QUESTION: What physical laws make a flying ball follow a curved path?
Core science
Projectile motion describes the movement of an object after launch when gravity governs its motion, under the assumption that air resistance is negligible.

For our ideal projectile:
- The horizontal acceleration is zero.
- The vertical acceleration is constant and points downward.
- The horizontal velocity remains constant.
- The vertical velocity changes continuously.
- Both horizontal and vertical motions occur over the same elapsed time.
Choose a coordinate system where positive x points right and positive y points upward.

The acceleration vector is:
\[
\vec a=-g\hat j
\]

Where, near Earth's surface:
\[
g\approx9.8\text{ m/s}^2
\]

We're treating gravity as constant over the relatively small height of the projectile's trajectory.
Narration idea
Once the ball leaves your hand, what controls its motion?

In our simplified model, we'll ignore air resistance.

That means gravity is the only force acting on the ball after release.

Gravity acts downward, so it changes the vertical component of velocity.

But it doesn't produce a horizontal acceleration in this model.

That gives us two different kinds of motion happening simultaneously: constant horizontal velocity and changing vertical velocity.


Topic 7.2 — Initial Launch Velocity
MAIN QUESTION: How do launch speed and launch angle determine the projectile's initial velocity?
Core mathematics
Let the initial speed be \(v_0\), with launch angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal.

From vector resolution:
\[
\boxed{v_{0x}=v_0\cos\theta}
\]

And:
\[
\boxed{v_{0y}=v_0\sin\theta}
\]

The complete initial velocity vector is:
\[
\vec v_0=v_0\cos\theta\,\hat i+v_0\sin\theta\,\hat j
\]

Worked example
For the rest of the chapter, use the same reference experiment:
\[
v_0=20\text{ m/s}
\]

\[
\theta=45^\circ
\]

The horizontal component is:
\[
v_{0x}=20\cos45^\circ\approx14.14\text{ m/s}
\]

The vertical component is:
\[
v_{0y}=20\sin45^\circ\approx14.14\text{ m/s}
\]

Together, these two components completely describe the projectile's initial velocity.
Narration idea
When you launch a ball at an angle, the initial velocity can be separated into two components.

One points horizontally, and the other points vertically.

Both come from the same initial velocity vector.

But after launch, gravity affects the vertical component while leaving the horizontal component unchanged in our ideal model.

That difference is what creates the curved trajectory.


Topic 7.3 — Horizontal Motion
MAIN QUESTION: Why does a projectile keep moving horizontally even though gravity pulls it downward?
Core science
When we neglect air resistance, no horizontal force acts on the projectile.

Therefore:
\[
a_x=0
\]

Its horizontal velocity remains constant:
\[
\boxed{v_x=v_0\cos\theta}
\]

The horizontal position as a function of time is:
\[
\boxed{x(t)=x_0+v_0\cos\theta\,t}
\]

If we choose the launch position as the origin:
\[
x(t)=v_0\cos\theta\,t
\]

For our reference example:
\[
x(t)\approx14.14t
\]

After one second:
\[
x(1)\approx14.14\text{ m}
\]

After two seconds:
\[
x(2)\approx28.28\text{ m}
\]

This means the projectile covers equal horizontal distances in equal time intervals.
Narration idea
Although gravity is pulling the ball downward, that doesn't mean it's directly slowing the horizontal motion.

In the absence of air resistance, the horizontal component of velocity remains constant.

Every second, the ball covers the same horizontal distance.

This part of the motion is straightforward.

The vertical component, however, follows a very different pattern.


Topic 7.4 — Vertical Motion
MAIN QUESTION: Why does a projectile slow down while rising and speed up while falling?
Core science
Take upward as the positive y-direction.

Gravity produces a constant downward acceleration:
\[
a_y=-g
\]

The vertical velocity is:
\[
\boxed{v_y(t)=v_0\sin\theta-gt}
\]

The vertical position is:
\[
\boxed{y(t)=y_0+v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2}
\]

For the ball launched from ground level:
\[
y(t)\approx14.14t-4.9t^2
\]

Its vertical velocity is:
\[
v_y(t)\approx14.14-9.8t
\]

During ascent, the vertical velocity is positive but decreases continuously.

At the maximum height, it reaches zero.

During descent, vertical velocity becomes negative, and the downward speed increases.
Narration idea
While the ball is rising, gravity pulls in the opposite direction to its vertical velocity.

So the upward motion gradually slows.

Eventually, the vertical velocity reaches zero, and the ball reaches its highest point.

After that, it begins to fall.

Gravity hasn't changed direction at any stage.

What changes is the direction and magnitude of the ball's vertical velocity.


Topic 7.5 — What Happens at the Highest Point?
MAIN QUESTION: Is a projectile completely stationary when it reaches the top of its trajectory?
Core science
At the highest point:
\[
v_y=0
\]

But its horizontal velocity is still:
\[
v_x=v_0\cos\theta
\]

For a nonvertical launch, the ball therefore continues moving horizontally.

Its acceleration remains:
\[
\vec a=-g\hat j
\]

This gives us four important observations:
- Vertical velocity is zero.
- Horizontal velocity is generally nonzero.
- Total speed is generally nonzero.
- Downward acceleration remains approximately 9.8 m/s².
For our 20 m/s launch at 45°, the speed at the highest point is approximately 14.14 m/s.
Narration idea
Here's a question that often causes confusion.

At the highest point of its flight, has the ball stopped moving?

Its vertical velocity has reached zero, but its horizontal velocity is still present.

So the ball hasn't stopped completely.

And gravity is still accelerating it downward.

This tells us something important: zero velocity in one direction does not imply zero acceleration.


Topic 7.6 — Why Is the Trajectory a Parabola?
MAIN QUESTION: Can we derive the exact shape of the projectile's path from the equations of motion?
Core mathematics
Choose the launch point as the origin, so x_0 = y_0 = 0. Assume a nonzero horizontal velocity component. Start with the horizontal position equation:
\[
x=v_0\cos\theta\,t
\]

Solve for time:
\[
t=\frac{x}{v_0\cos\theta}
\]

This assumes the projectile has a nonzero horizontal velocity component.

Now substitute this expression into the vertical position equation:
\[
y=v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2
\]

After simplifying:
\[
\boxed{y=x\tan\theta-\frac{gx^2}{2v_0^2\cos^2\theta}}
\]

Notice that this equation has the mathematical form:
\[
y=Ax-Bx^2
\]

That's a quadratic function of x.

Therefore, the trajectory is a parabola.

The important message here is that the parabolic path isn't something we assumed beforehand.

It emerges naturally from combining constant horizontal velocity with constant vertical acceleration.
Narration idea
We've seen that a projectile follows a curved path.

But why does that curve have the shape of a parabola?

We didn't begin by assuming the answer.

We started with the laws governing horizontal and vertical motion.

When we combine those equations and eliminate time, a quadratic relationship appears.

The parabolic trajectory is a mathematical consequence of the physics.

Chapter takeaway: Projectile motion combines constant horizontal velocity with constant downward acceleration. Together, they produce the familiar parabolic trajectory.

Chapter 8 — Flight Time, Maximum Height, and Range
34:27–39:02

For this section, we'll assume that the projectile launches and lands at the same height, experiences constant gravity, and moves without air resistance.
Topic 8.1 — Time of Flight
MAIN QUESTION: How can we calculate how long a projectile remains in the air?
Core mathematics
The vertical position relative to the launch point is:
\[
y(t)=v_0\sin\theta\,t-\frac12gt^2
\]

When the projectile returns to its launch height:
\[
y(T)=0
\]

Therefore:
\[
0=v_0\sin\theta\,T-\frac12gT^2
\]

Factor the expression:
\[
0=T\left(v_0\sin\theta-\frac12gT\right)
\]

The first solution, \(T=0\), corresponds to the instant of launch.

The second solution gives the landing time:
\[
\boxed{T=\frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}}
\]

For the reference example:
\[
T\approx2.89\text{ s}
\]

Narration idea
How long will our ball remain in the air?

To answer that, we need to determine when it returns to its original height.

The vertical motion equation allows us to calculate that time.

Under our ideal assumptions, the time spent rising is equal to the time spent falling back to the same height.

That symmetry gives us a simple expression for the total flight time.


Topic 8.2 — Maximum Height
MAIN QUESTION: How can we determine the highest point a projectile will reach?
Core mathematics
We know that the vertical velocity is zero at the maximum height:
\[
v_y=0
\]

Start from:
\[
v_y=v_0\sin\theta-gt
\]

Solving for the time to maximum height gives:
\[
t_H=\frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}
\]

Substituting this into the vertical displacement equation leads to:
\[
\boxed{H=\frac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta}{2g}}
\]

For the 20 m/s launch at 45°:
\[
H=\frac{20^2\sin^245^\circ}{2(9.8)}
\]

Therefore:
\[
\boxed{H\approx10.20\text{ m}}
\]

This is the maximum height measured above the launch position.
Narration idea
Maximum height is determined by how much upward velocity the projectile has at launch.

For the same initial speed and an angle between 0° and 90° above the horizontal, a steeper launch gives a larger vertical velocity component.

That allows the ball to rise higher before gravity reduces its upward velocity to zero.

So launch angle doesn't just influence where the ball lands. It also determines how high the ball travels.


Topic 8.3 — Horizontal Range
MAIN QUESTION: Can we predict the horizontal distance between launch and landing?
Core mathematics
Because horizontal velocity is constant:
\[
R=v_0\cos\theta\,T
\]

Substitute the expression for flight time:
\[
R=v_0\cos\theta\left(\frac{2v_0\sin\theta}{g}\right)
\]

Now use the identity:
\[
2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta
\]

We obtain:
\[
\boxed{R=\frac{v_0^2\sin2\theta}{g}}
\]

For the reference experiment:
\[
R=\frac{20^2\sin90^\circ}{9.8}
\]

So:
\[
\boxed{R\approx40.82\text{ m}}
\]

Narration idea
To travel a long horizontal distance, a projectile needs two things.

First, it needs enough horizontal velocity to move forward.

Second, it needs sufficient time in the air.

A shallow launch gives more horizontal velocity but less flight time.

A steep launch gives more flight time but less horizontal velocity.

The range depends on how those two effects combine.


Topic 8.4 — Why 45° Gives Maximum Range
MAIN QUESTION: Why does a launch angle of 45° maximize range in ideal projectile motion?
Core science and math
Recall the range equation:
\[
R=\frac{v_0^2}{g}\sin2\theta
\]

If the initial speed and gravity are fixed, the only variable affecting the range is:
\[
\sin2\theta
\]

The largest possible value of sine is 1.

Therefore:
\[
\sin2\theta=1
\]

For launch angles between 0° and 90°:
\[
2\theta=90^\circ
\]

And hence:
\[
\boxed{\theta=45^\circ}
\]

So, for an ideal projectile launched and landing at the same height, 45° produces the greatest horizontal range.

There's another interesting mathematical result here.

Complementary angles, such as 30° and 60°, produce the same range because they give the same value of \(\sin2\theta\).
Numerical comparison
For an initial speed of 20 m/s:
Launch angle	Maximum height	Flight time	Horizontal range
30°	5.10 m	2.04 s	35.35 m
45°	10.20 m	2.89 s	40.82 m
60°	15.31 m	3.53 s	35.35 m

Important scientific limitation
Don't present 45° as a universal rule.

It gives the maximum range only under the assumptions used in this derivation.

If the projectile lands at a different height from where it launches, or if air resistance is significant, the angle that maximizes the range can be different.

The mathematical expression above assumes constant gravity, equal launch and landing heights, and no air resistance.
Narration idea
The 45-degree launch angle creates a balance between horizontal velocity and flight time.

A lower angle produces more forward velocity, but the projectile returns to the ground sooner.

A higher angle keeps the projectile in the air longer, but reduces how quickly it travels horizontally.

Under our ideal assumptions, these competing effects produce the greatest range at 45 degrees.

In real situations, however, air resistance and differences in launch and landing height can change the optimum angle.

The equations don't just give us answers. They also tell us the conditions under which those answers are valid.

Chapter takeaway: Once we know the initial speed, launch angle, and gravitational acceleration, we can predict how long an ideal projectile stays in the air, how high it rises, and how far it travels.

Topic 9.1 — Test the Physical Intuition
MAIN QUESTION: Can you reason about motion using the concepts you've learned, rather than simply recalling formulas?

These questions help reinforce the difference between memorizing equations and understanding what they mean physically.

Question 1: A projectile reaches the highest point of its trajectory. What happens to its vertical velocity and acceleration?

Correct interpretation: The vertical velocity becomes zero momentarily, but the downward acceleration remains constant.

Question 2: A ball is dropped from a height while another ball is launched horizontally from the same height at the same instant. Which ball reaches the ground first?

Correct interpretation: Both reach the ground simultaneously if they start at the same time and height, have zero initial vertical velocity, and experience the same constant downward acceleration without air resistance.

Question 3: Two projectiles are launched with equal speeds at angles of 30° and 60°, and land at the same height. Which travels farther horizontally?

Correct interpretation: They have identical ranges in the ideal model, even though their maximum heights and flight times differ.

Question 4: An object moves with a constant speed but continuously changes direction. Is it accelerating?

Correct interpretation: Yes. Acceleration measures changes in the velocity vector, including changes in direction.

Question 5: If the launch speed of a projectile doubles while its angle remains constant, what happens to its ideal horizontal range?

Correct interpretation: The range becomes four times larger because:
\[
R\propto v_0^2
\]

This assumes identical launch and landing heights and no air resistance.
