When we hear the word acceleration, we usually picture something speeding up. Physics uses the word more precisely. Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. Average acceleration is delta v over delta t. Instantaneous acceleration is d v d t. And since velocity is the derivative of position, acceleration is the second derivative: d squared r over d t squared. So when is something accelerating? Watch four cars. One speeds up. Acceleration points forward, along the velocity. One brakes. Acceleration points backward, against the velocity. Slowing down is acceleration too. One turns at constant speed. Acceleration points sideways, toward the inside of the turn. And one turns while speeding up. Acceleration points partly forward, partly inward. Over an interval, delta v gives the average acceleration's direction. At an instant, use the limiting change in velocity. So an object moving at constant speed can still have an acceleration. The only way to have zero acceleration is to keep both speed and direction unchanged. Keep that in mind. It will matter at the highest point of a projectile's flight.
