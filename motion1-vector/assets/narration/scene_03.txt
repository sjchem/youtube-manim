Some quantities need only a number and a unit. Five kilograms of mass. Ten seconds of time. Twenty-five degrees Celsius. A hundred metres of distance. Twenty metres per second of speed. Fifty joules of energy. These are scalars: quantities described by a numerical value and an appropriate unit, without a direction in space. Scalars aren't always positive. Minus five degrees Celsius is still a scalar. The minus sign means colder than zero, not a direction you could point to. Speed is a scalar too. It tells us how quickly distance is covered. Average speed is the total distance divided by the elapsed time. A runner covers one hundred metres in ten seconds. One hundred divided by ten: an average speed of ten metres per second. Now think of a car's speedometer. It reads twenty metres per second. But from that reading alone, can you tell whether the car is heading north, south, east, or west? You can't. Mass or temperature needs a value and a unit. To describe a velocity, we also need one more piece of information. Direction.
