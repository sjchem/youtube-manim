Look at the range equation again. With speed and gravity fixed, only sine two theta can change. Sine can never be bigger than one. It reaches one when two theta is ninety degrees. So theta is forty-five degrees: the greatest range, for an ideal projectile landing at its launch height. Sweep through every angle, and watch the landing point. There's a bonus. Thirty and sixty degrees reach the same range, about thirty-five point three five metres away, but land at different times. Sine of sixty and sine of one hundred and twenty are equal. Complementary angles give equal ranges. The thirty-degree throw peaks at five point one zero metres, landing after two point zero four seconds. The sixty-degree throw reaches fifteen point three one metres, and takes three point five three seconds. Different heights, different flight times, same range. But don't treat forty-five degrees as a universal rule. In this still-air drag model, the flight loses symmetry and lands short. For this ball, the best angle drops below forty-five degrees. Launch from a height, like a ball thrown off a ten metre ledge, and the best angle is lower too: about thirty-nine degrees. The equations don't just give answers. They tell us when those answers apply.
