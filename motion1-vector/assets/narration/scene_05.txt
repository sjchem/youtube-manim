Can you travel a long way and still have zero displacement? Let's find out. Distance keeps track of the whole path you travel. Displacement asks a different question: where did you finish, compared with where you started? Written as a vector: delta r equals r final minus r initial. Walk three metres east. Then four metres north. The distance is easy. Three plus four: seven metres. But the displacement is the straight arrow from start to finish. Its length comes from Pythagoras: the square root of three squared plus four squared. Five metres. Its direction: the angle whose tangent is four over three, about fifty-three point one degrees north of east. Seven metres travelled. Five metres of displacement. Same journey, two different questions. Now run one lap of a four hundred metre track. Watch both numbers as you go. The distance only ever grows. The displacement arrow stretches out, swings around, and then shrinks as you come home. Back at the start: four hundred metres of distance. Zero displacement. Scalars describe magnitude. Vectors describe magnitude and direction. And distance and displacement measure two different things about the same trip.
