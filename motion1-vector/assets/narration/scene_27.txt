Time to test your intuition. Five questions. Pause if you like. One. At the top of its flight, what are a projectile's vertical velocity and acceleration? Vertical velocity: momentarily zero. Acceleration: still g, straight down. Two. Ignore air resistance. One ball is dropped; another launches horizontally from the same height at the same instant. Which lands first? They land together. Both start with zero vertical velocity, and gravity treats them identically. The horizontal motion doesn't matter. Three. Same launch speed, thirty and sixty degrees, same landing height, no air resistance. Which travels farther? Neither. In the ideal model, their ranges are identical, even though their heights and flight times differ. Four. An object moves at constant speed, but keeps changing direction. Is it accelerating? Yes. Acceleration measures any change in the velocity vector, including a change of direction. Five. If you double the launch speed and keep the angle, what happens to the range? It quadruples. Range grows with v zero squared, as long as launch and landing heights match and air resistance is negligible. If a question surprised you, replay its demonstration. Explaining the arrows in your own words is how understanding grows.
