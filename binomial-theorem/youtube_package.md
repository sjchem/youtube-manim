## Description

Expand (a+b)⁵ and you get 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1. Row 5 of Pascal's triangle —
built only by adding neighbours — says exactly the same thing. Coincidence?

In this video we stop multiplying and start choosing. Every bracket picks
either a or b; every term of the expansion is a list of choices; and every
coefficient counts how many lists land on that term. That turns the binomial
theorem into a counting problem — and shows why the binomial coefficients,
combinations and Pascal's triangle are one structure seen from three sides.
Along the way: (a+b)² as an area, (a+b)³ as a 3D cube, two proofs of Pascal's
rule, paths through the triangle, coin flips, and the bell curve.

Notes on accuracy: the choice machines are a picture of the distributive law,
not a physical claim. The Galton board is idealised as independent fair
bounces; the 64-ball sample is real (seeded) randomness and wobbles around the
exact 2·(1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1). The bell curve is the normal approximation to the
binomial row (mean n/2, variance n/4). The 11ⁿ digit trick only works while
every entry of the row is a single digit (up to row 4).
