## Scene 01 — The mystery — 00:00–01:04

Why does Pascal's triangle know algebra? Expand a plus b to the fifth: six terms, six coefficients. Those numbers are the coefficients. Where do they come from? One way is to multiply it out. Five copies of a plus b, multiplied together. The products pile up: two, four, eight, sixteen, thirty-two, before anything simplifies. Then like terms collapse together, and six coefficients survive. One, five, ten, ten, five, one. In Pascal's triangle, call the top row zero. Row five reads one, five, ten, ten, five, one. The same six numbers. Exactly. Why would a triangle built by simple addition know the answer to an algebra problem? That's our mystery. The answer is a story about choices. The binomial theorem, explained visually.

## Scene 02 — Start small: squares and rectangles — 01:04–02:40

Let's not start with the fifth power. Start small, and watch what happens. a plus b to the power zero is just one. One term, coefficient one. To the first power, it's a plus b itself. Two terms, each with coefficient one. Now square it. Let a and b be positive lengths making up this square's side. Split each side into a and b. The square breaks into four regions. A square of side a: that's a squared. A square of side b: b squared. And two rectangles, each a by b. One stands up, one lies down, but their areas are equal: a times b. Slide them together. Two copies of a b. So a plus b, squared, is a squared, plus two a b, plus b squared. Coefficients one, two, one. The algebra agrees. Every term in the first bracket meets every term in the second. a times a. a times b. b times a. b times b. Four products. But a b and b a are the same product, just reordered. So they merge. That two isn't a rule to memorise. It counts something: two different ways to end up with one a and one b. Hold on to that idea. A coefficient counts the ways. For the cube, we need one more dimension.

## Scene 03 — The cube of a + b — 02:40–04:22

Build a cube whose edge is a plus b. Cut every edge into a piece a and a piece b, in all three directions. The cube splits into eight blocks. Pull them apart, so we can see them all. One big cube, a by a by a. That's a cubed. One small cube, b by b by b. That's b cubed. Then three flat slabs. Each is a by a by b: a squared b. Three of them. And three long beams, a by b by b: a b squared. Again, three. Sort the eight blocks by shape. One, three, three, one. So a plus b, cubed, is a cubed, plus three a squared b, plus three a b squared, plus b cubed. Why three slabs? A slab has one thin side, and that thin side can point along any of the three directions. Choose which direction gets the b. Three choices. Three slabs. A beam is the reverse. Two thin sides, so it chooses two of the three directions. Again, three ways. And eight blocks in all, because each direction makes its own choice, a or b. Two, times two, times two. Coefficients count choices again. But a fourth power would need a fourth dimension. So we need a better picture.

## Scene 04 — The triangle builds itself — 04:22–06:17

Let's line up what we've found, one power at a time. Power zero: one. Power one: one, one. Power two: one, two, one. Power three: one, three, three, one. Keep going. Power four gives one, four, six, four, one. And power five, one, five, ten, ten, five, one. Now strip away the algebra, and keep only the coefficients. We weren't building Pascal's triangle. We were expanding binomials. Yet somehow, the triangle built itself. The edges are always one. Inside, each number adds its two neighbours above: three plus three is six; four plus six is ten. But why should expansions obey that rule? Here's a clue. To reach the fifth power, multiply the fourth power by one more a plus b. Multiplying by a keeps every term, and adds one to each power of a. Multiplying by b does the same with b, and slides every term one place along. Now add the two copies. Terms with the same powers land on top of each other. One. Four plus one. Six plus four. Four plus six. One plus four. And one. One, five, ten, ten, five, one. Each new coefficient is the sum of two old ones: the rule of the triangle. So the algebra shows how the triangle grows. But not what its numbers mean. Why ten? To answer that, we have to stop multiplying, and start choosing.

## Scene 05 — Five choice machines — 06:17–08:25

Write a plus b to the fifth as what it really is: five brackets, multiplied together. Think of each bracket as a little machine with two doors. One door says a. The other says b. To make one term of the expansion, every machine opens exactly one door. Say the first picks a. The second, a. The third, b. The fourth, a. The fifth, b. Multiply what came out: a, a, b, a, b. Three a's and two b's. a cubed b squared. Every set of choices makes one product. How many products are there? Each machine has two options, chosen independently. Two times two times two times two times two: thirty-two. Here they are. All thirty-two ways the five machines can choose. These are exactly the thirty-two products brute force made. Now each one is a list of choices. Let's sort them. Not by order, but by how many b's each list contains. No b's at all: just one list. All a. Exactly one b: five lists. The b can come from any one of the five machines. Two b's: ten lists. Three b's: ten. Four b's: five. Five b's: one. Look at the piles. One, five, ten, ten, five, one. Every list in a pile multiplies to the same term. So the size of each pile is that term's coefficient. And all the piles together hold thirty-two lists. Two to the fifth. The triangle's row is the sizes of these piles. Expanding a binomial is secretly sorting choices. So the real question is: how do we count a pile, without listing it?

## Scene 06 — Combinations enter — 08:25–10:36

Take the pile for a cubed b squared. Each list in it has exactly two b's. Once you know where those two b's are, the rest is forced: everything else is a. So a list is just a choice of two machines out of five. Machines one and two. One and three. One and four. One and five. Two and three. Two and four. Two and five. Three and four. Three and five. And four and five. Ten ways to choose two of five. Ten lists. Ten copies of a cubed b squared. They collapse into a single term: ten a cubed b squared. Can we count them without listing? Pick a first b machine: five options. Then a second: four are left. Five times four is twenty. But that counts every pair twice. Choosing machine two, then four, gives the same list as four, then two. Only which machines say b matters. So divide by two. Twenty over two is ten. This count has a name: five choose two. The number of ways to choose two things from five, when order doesn't matter. For n brackets, choose k for b. Ordered picks count each group k factorial times, so divide by k factorial. The count is n choose k: n factorial, divided by k factorial and by the factorial of n minus k. Five factorial is one hundred twenty. Divide by two factorial times three factorial: two times six. Ten. Every coefficient is one of these counts. Five choose zero is one. Five choose one is five. Then ten, ten, five, and one. The coefficient isn't an arbitrary number. It's counting something. Now we can write the whole theorem, and every symbol will already mean something.

## Scene 07 — The binomial theorem — 10:36–12:15

Here it is: the binomial theorem. a plus b to the n equals the sum, for k from zero to n, of n choose k, a to the n minus k, b to the k. Read it from the inside out. b to the k: k of the brackets chose b. a to the n minus k: the rest chose a. n choose k: the number of ways to decide which brackets those are. And the sum: collect every possible number of b's, from none at all to every bracket. For n equal to zero, one, two, and so on: choose, count, collect. Let's test it. Set n to five, and let k run from zero to five. Each value of k gives one term: five choose k, times a to the five minus k, times b to the k. Evaluate each count. One, five, ten, ten, five, one. The expansion we started with, rebuilt without multiplying anything. And we never needed the triangle. The counts come straight from choosing. For power ten, the middle term is ten choose five: two hundred fifty-two a to the fifth b to the fifth. But look closer at the exponents. They move in a very particular way.

## Scene 08 — Why the exponents move oppositely — 12:15–13:56

Watch the powers march across the expansion. The powers of a count down: five, four, three, two, one, zero. The powers of b count up: zero, one, two, three, four, five. Why do they move in opposite directions? Because there are always five choices in total. Five machines, five letters. Every product has five factors. Start with all a. Now flip one machine from a to b. One a disappears; one b appears. Flip another. And another. Each step trades one a for one b. The two exponents always add up to five. Two sides of one budget. That trade gives the triangle another property. Swap the roles of a and b, and the row reverses. Choosing which two machines say b is the same as choosing which three say a. So five choose two equals five choose three. Ten equals ten. That's the mirror symmetry of every row. In general, n choose k equals n choose n minus k. Picking what goes in is picking what stays out. And the sum of a row? Set a and b to one. Each term becomes its coefficient, and the left side becomes two to the n. One plus five plus ten plus ten plus five plus one: thirty-two. Every choice list, counted once. Symmetry and sums are nice. But why does adding two neighbours give the number below?

## Scene 09 — Why Pascal's rule works — 13:56–15:47

Pascal's rule says ten is four plus six. In counting language: five choose two equals four choose one plus four choose two. Let's see why, with our ten lists. Each list picks two of the five machines for b. Now focus on just one machine: the last one. Either the last machine says b, or it says a. There's no third option. So split the ten lists into two groups. Group one: the last machine says b. Then just one more b is needed, from the first four machines. Four choose one: four ways. Group two: the last machine says a. Then both b's come from the first four. Four choose two: six ways. Four plus six. Ten. Every list lands in exactly one group. Nothing counted twice, nothing missed. The same argument works anywhere. To choose k from n, look at the newest item. Either it's chosen, and you need k minus one more from the rest, or it isn't, and you need all k from the rest. n choose k equals n minus one choose k minus one, plus n minus one choose k. That's the triangle's addition rule. Not a coincidence of arithmetic, but a way of sorting choices by one question: in or out? And the shifted copies from the algebra? Times a: the new bracket says a. Times b: it says b. The same split, in algebra's clothes. Two arguments, one structure. Next, a third way to see these numbers: as paths.

## Scene 10 — Paths through the triangle — 15:47–17:37

Turn the triangle into a little maze. Start at the top. At every row, step down to the left, or down to the right. Call a left step a, and a right step b. Five steps down, and you reach row five. Your path is a list of five letters, a's and b's, just like our choice machines. Here's one path: a, b, a, a, b. It ends two places from the left, because exactly two steps went right. Where you land depends only on how many b's you took. Not on their order. So how many paths reach this spot, the ten? Any path with two right steps among its five. That's five choose two. Watch them arrive. All ten paths, layered on top of each other. Every number in the triangle counts the paths that reach it. And Pascal's rule appears a third time. Every path into this spot comes through one of the two spots above it. Four paths arrive from the upper left. Six from the upper right. Four plus six, ten. Paths, choices, coefficients. Three different stories, and the same numbers in every one. Pascal's triangle, combinations, binomial coefficients. Not three facts that happen to agree, but one structure seen from three sides. Now let's make the walk random.

## Scene 11 — Coin flips and the bell curve — 17:37–19:33

Flip a fair coin independently at every step. Heads, go left. Tails, go right. Five flips make one path. Our ideal board treats each bounce as an independent fair flip. Drop a ball. Another. Thirty-two possible sequences, and for a fair coin each one is equally likely. How many sequences have exactly two tails? Five choose two. Ten. So the chance of exactly two tails is ten out of thirty-two. Just under a third. The whole row tells the whole story: one, five, ten, ten, five, one, out of thirty-two. ## Scene 12 — The final payoff — 19:33–21:13

Back to where we started: a plus b, to the fifth power. Five brackets. Each one chooses a or b. For each number of b's, count the ways to pick those brackets. No b's: one way. One b: five ways. Two b's: ten. Three: ten. Four: five. Five b's: one. Attach the powers: a counts down, b counts up. And the whole expansion appears, without multiplying out the brackets. Here's the picture behind it. Expanding is making choices. Counting the choices gives combinations. Combinations are the binomial coefficients. And they stack, row by row, into Pascal's triangle. So Pascal's triangle doesn't magically predict the binomial theorem. Both grow from the same counting structure. One last trick. Set a to ten, and b to one. Then a plus b to the fourth is eleven to the fourth. Row four is one, four, six, four, one. And eleven to the fourth is fourteen thousand, six hundred forty-one. At row five, coefficients reach ten. Carrying gives eleven to the fifth: one hundred sixty-one thousand, fifty-one. The binomial theorem works because algebraic expansion secretly counts combinations. Every coefficient is a number of ways to choose.
