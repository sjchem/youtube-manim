Back to where we started: a plus b, to the fifth power. Five brackets. Each one chooses a or b. For each number of b's, count the ways to pick those brackets. No b's: one way. One b: five ways. Two b's: ten. Three: ten. Four: five. Five b's: one. Attach the powers: a counts down, b counts up. And the whole expansion appears, without multiplying out the brackets. Here's the picture behind it. Expanding is making choices. Counting the choices gives combinations. Combinations are the binomial coefficients. And they stack, row by row, into Pascal's triangle. So Pascal's triangle doesn't magically predict the binomial theorem. Both grow from the same counting structure. One last trick. Set a to ten, and b to one. Then a plus b to the fourth is eleven to the fourth. Row four is one, four, six, four, one. And eleven to the fourth is fourteen thousand, six hundred forty-one. At row five, coefficients reach ten. Carrying gives eleven to the fifth: one hundred sixty-one thousand, fifty-one. The binomial theorem works because algebraic expansion secretly counts combinations. Every coefficient is a number of ways to choose.
