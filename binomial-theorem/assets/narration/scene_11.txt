Flip a fair coin independently at every step. Heads, go left. Tails, go right. Five flips make one path. Our ideal board treats each bounce as an independent fair flip. Drop a ball. Another. And now, many more. They pile up in the middle, and rarely at the edges. Why? Use H and T as labels: H plus T, to the fifth, counts the five-flip sequences. Thirty-two possible sequences, and for a fair coin each one is equally likely. How many sequences have exactly two tails? Five choose two. Ten. So the chance of exactly two tails is ten out of thirty-two. Just under a third. The whole row tells the whole story: one, five, ten, ten, five, one, out of thirty-two. Lift that row out of the triangle, and its numbers become the heights of a histogram. Gold shows expected counts for sixty-four balls. Blue shows our sample. With more balls, the proportions tend to get closer. With more flips, the rows grow longer. Ten flips: the middle bar is two hundred fifty-two, out of one thousand and twenty-four. For twenty flips, a smooth bell curve already approximates the bars. That bell is a doorway to probability and statistics. And it was hiding inside a binomial all along. The same counting structure, now measuring chance.
