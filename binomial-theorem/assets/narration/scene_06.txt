Take the pile for a cubed b squared. Each list in it has exactly two b's. Once you know where those two b's are, the rest is forced: everything else is a. So a list is just a choice of two machines out of five. Machines one and two. One and three. One and four. One and five. Two and three. Two and four. Two and five. Three and four. Three and five. And four and five. Ten ways to choose two of five. Ten lists. Ten copies of a cubed b squared. They collapse into a single term: ten a cubed b squared. Can we count them without listing? Pick a first b machine: five options. Then a second: four are left. Five times four is twenty. But that counts every pair twice. Choosing machine two, then four, gives the same list as four, then two. Only which machines say b matters. So divide by two. Twenty over two is ten. This count has a name: five choose two. The number of ways to choose two things from five, when order doesn't matter. For n brackets, choose k for b. Ordered picks count each group k factorial times, so divide by k factorial. The count is n choose k: n factorial, divided by k factorial and by the factorial of n minus k. Five factorial is one hundred twenty. Divide by two factorial times three factorial: two times six. Ten. Every coefficient is one of these counts. Five choose zero is one. Five choose one is five. Then ten, ten, five, and one. The coefficient isn't an arbitrary number. It's counting something. Now we can write the whole theorem, and every symbol will already mean something.
