Pascal's rule says ten is four plus six. In counting language: five choose two equals four choose one plus four choose two. Let's see why, with our ten lists. Each list picks two of the five machines for b. Now focus on just one machine: the last one. Either the last machine says b, or it says a. There's no third option. So split the ten lists into two groups. Group one: the last machine says b. Then just one more b is needed, from the first four machines. Four choose one: four ways. Group two: the last machine says a. Then both b's come from the first four. Four choose two: six ways. Four plus six. Ten. Every list lands in exactly one group. Nothing counted twice, nothing missed. The same argument works anywhere. To choose k from n, look at the newest item. Either it's chosen, and you need k minus one more from the rest, or it isn't, and you need all k from the rest. n choose k equals n minus one choose k minus one, plus n minus one choose k. That's the triangle's addition rule. Not a coincidence of arithmetic, but a way of sorting choices by one question: in or out? And the shifted copies from the algebra? Times a: the new bracket says a. Times b: it says b. The same split, in algebra's clothes. Two arguments, one structure. Next, a third way to see these numbers: as paths.
