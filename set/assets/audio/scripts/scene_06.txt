Our friends have written two topping orders. Order A asks for mushroom and olive. Order B asks for olive and pepper. Suppose we want a shopping list covering either order. We collect mushroom, olive, and pepper. Olive appears on both orders, but it only needs one entry in our set of topping names. This collection is the union of A and B. The cup-shaped symbol means union, and the rule is simple: belonging to either set is enough. Here, “or” includes belonging to both. Now suppose the friends want a topping they both chose. That is a different question. Imagine checking the topping against two order stations. First it must appear on A, and then it must appear on B. Follow the mushroom. It passes the first station but fails the second, so it is not shared by both orders. Pepper fails the first station, even though it would pass the second. Olive passes both. It is the topping that both friends selected. This shared collection is the intersection, written with the cap-shaped symbol. Union collects from either order. Intersection keeps only what is common to both. Notice that our symbols describe actions we already understand from shopping and sharing. If two orders had no topping in common, their intersection would be the empty set. That would still be a perfectly meaningful answer. Keep those two questions in mind as we change the objects from ingredients to numbers. Let x stand for any topping. The equation for union says x belongs to A union B exactly when it belongs to A or B, including both. The equation for intersection changes that or to and. These are membership tests that work for any sets, not just these two orders.
