For the second law, imagine a toy gate with one exact rule: block a toy when it is red and square. Here are four toys: red or blue, square or round. Which can pass? The red square is blocked because it meets both conditions. The red ball passes because it is not square. The blue square passes because it is not red. The blue ball passes because it fails both blocking conditions. To pass the gate, a toy therefore needs to be not red or not square. Either is enough, and satisfying both exclusions is allowed. Now draw red toys as one set and square toys as the other. The blocked region is their intersection. Its complement contains everything except that shared region. The same selection is the union of the two complements: outside the red set or outside the square set. That is De Morgan's second law. Negating “and” gives “or” between the negated conditions. Not both does not mean neither; a toy may still meet one of the original conditions. The same membership test proves the second law. If x is not in the intersection, then x fails membership in R or fails membership in S. So the complement of the intersection equals the union of the complements.
