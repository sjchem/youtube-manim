From period two onward, each row starts a fresh outer shell. Its first element has n s one: a single electron in that new shell. The row ends with closed outer s and p subshells: n s two, n p six. Helium closes one s. Follow lithium across to neon. Then sodium starts again, and argon closes the next row. Potassium begins another cycle, ending at krypton. Fill available states, close the outer s and p set, and start again. Because the valence patterns recur, broad chemical behaviour recurs too: a reactive metal, changing properties, then a usually unreactive noble gas. That is periodicity. Similar patterns return at intervals as atomic number increases. The row lengths are two, eight, eight, eighteen, eighteen, thirty-two and thirty-two. The intervals are unequal. Two s places and six p places give eight. That recipe repeats in periods two and three. Add ten d places and the count becomes eighteen. Those d states belong to the previous principal shell. Fourteen f places give thirty-two. A period can fill several shells; its length follows the subshell sequence.
