Our four orbital families are s, p, d and f. Their quantum labels tell us how many independent orbitals each subshell contains. The s family is spherically symmetric. An s subshell contains one orbital. This picture shows its angular shape, without radial detail. Pauli exclusion allows at most two electrons in one spatial orbital, with opposite spins. One orbital, two spin states. An s subshell holds two electrons. A p subshell contains three orbitals. We can picture them as dumbbells pointing along three perpendicular directions. Three orbitals, two electrons each: a capacity of six. A d subshell contains five orbitals. Four have cloverlike shapes; the fifth has two lobes and a ring. Five orbitals, two electrons each: ten places altogether. The f family has more complicated angular shapes, and seven independent orbitals. Seven orbitals give fourteen electron places in an f subshell. So our four capacities are two, six, ten and fourteen. The general count is two l plus one orbitals. For s, p, d and f, l is zero, one, two and three. Now turn each electron place into a column of elements. A quantum count becomes a visible width. Two places make the two-column s block. Six make the p block. Ten make the d block. The middle of the table already has an explanation. Fourteen make the f strip: seven orbitals with two spin states each. Bring these four blocks together in the familiar layout. There it is: capacity sets the width. The block sizes follow from quantum states. Chemists choose how to arrange the diagram on a page, but these capacities constrain its structure. Let us build the first rows and watch the pattern emerge.
