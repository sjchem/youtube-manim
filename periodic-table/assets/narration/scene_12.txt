Colour the whole table by its four structural blocks, keeping the colours of the orbital boxes. Blue marks the s region: two columns. Green marks the p region: six columns. Gold marks the d region: ten columns. Purple marks the f region: fourteen columns. Helium stays blue because its configuration is one s squared, even though its chemistry places it above the noble gases. Raise the blocks. Their heights now echo the same capacities as their widths. Above each block, bring back a representative orbital shape. A sphere for s: one orbital per subshell. A dumbbell for p: three independent orbitals. A cloverlike example for d: five orbitals. And a more complex f shape: seven orbitals. Two spin states turn one, three, five and seven orbitals into two, six, ten and fourteen places. The widths, the gaps in the early rows, and the strips underneath all fit the same story: capacities and the order of filling. But why does the story start again at each new row? That is where the word periodic comes from.
