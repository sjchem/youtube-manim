Lithium has three electrons. Two occupy one s, making a closed core like helium. The remaining electron enters two s, outside that core. Place lithium at the beginning of period two, below hydrogen. Beryllium adds a second two s electron, with opposite spin. That subshell is full. Now the two p subshell becomes available: three orbitals, six places. Boron has one electron in two p. It begins the p block. Carbon adds another. Among these equal-energy p orbitals, the ground state prefers separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. That is Hund's rule. Nitrogen gives us one electron in each of the three p orbitals. Oxygen adds a pair. Fluorine adds another. The empty orbitals have run out. Neon completes the final pair: two p to the sixth. Together with two s squared, shell two is full. Look back at the row. Filling two s gave two elements; filling two p gave six. Two plus six is eight. That is the length of period two. The s elements sit on the left and the p elements on the right. Their shared families give the row its two distinct regions. The gap between them reserves space for the d block. The short early rows have not reached it yet. Period three repeats the same count: sodium and magnesium fill three s, then aluminium through argon fill three p. Eight elements again. Compare lithium and sodium, looking beyond their closed cores. Lithium is a helium core plus two s to the one. Sodium is a neon core plus three s to the one. The shell number changes. The outer pattern does not: one s electron in a new shell. That repeating pattern places sodium below lithium. It also suggests why they share related chemistry. Now the vertical columns begin to make sense.
