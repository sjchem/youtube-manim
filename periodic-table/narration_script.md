## Scene 01 — Why this shape? — 00:00–00:50

This is one of the most recognizable pictures in science: the periodic table. But look at its outline. Why does it have this strange shape? Two columns on the left. Ten in the middle. Six on the right. And two rows of fourteen positions tucked underneath. Why separate them? Why these widths? And why do elements directly above each other often share similar chemistry? ## Scene 02 — Atomic number gives the order — 00:50–02:00

To rebuild it, start at the centre of an atom: the nucleus. One proton identifies hydrogen. That is the first element. Two protons identify helium. Changing the proton count changes the element itself. Six protons identify carbon, whether it is in a diamond, a leaf or your body. And seventy-nine identify gold. Each element has its own proton count. That count is the atomic number, written Z. Neutrons can change the isotope; electrons can change the charge. Protons decide the element. So place the elements in order of increasing Z. One, two, three, four. For now, we get a single line. Hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium, and onward through the hundred and eighteen elements in the familiar table. We know the order. But why unequal rows, gaps, and columns of similar elements? Wrapping it every eighteen places gives the wrong outline. To discover the row lengths, we need to look outside the nucleus, at the electrons.

## Scene 03 — The real puzzle is the electrons — 02:00–03:15

We build neutral atoms in their ground states, with the same number of electrons as protons. The next element has one more proton and one more electron. Its positive and negative charges still balance. Hydrogen has one electron, helium two, lithium three, and beryllium four. The count rises with atomic number. The new proton belongs in the nucleus. The new electron raises the question that will shape the entire table. Where can it go? What states are still available? In Part Two, we replaced planetary orbits with orbitals: quantum states that describe electron probability. Related orbitals form a subshell. The ground configuration minimizes the energy of the whole atom, including interactions between electrons. The usual filling sequence is a useful first guide. These boxes count available orbitals; they are not physical compartments inside an atom. As we fill them, we also count elements. But the number of boxes differs between subshells. Why? That difference gives us the four capacities, and the four widths of the periodic table.

## Scene 04 — The four shapes that build the table — 03:15–05:30

Our four orbital families are s, p, d and f. Their quantum labels tell us how many independent orbitals each subshell contains. The s family is spherically symmetric. An s subshell contains one orbital. This picture shows its angular shape, without radial detail. Pauli exclusion allows at most two electrons in one spatial orbital, with opposite spins. One orbital, two spin states. An s subshell holds two electrons. A p subshell contains three orbitals. We can picture them as dumbbells pointing along three perpendicular directions. Three orbitals, two electrons each: a capacity of six. A d subshell contains five orbitals. Four have cloverlike shapes; the fifth has two lobes and a ring. Five orbitals, two electrons each: ten places altogether. The f family has more complicated angular shapes, and seven independent orbitals. Seven orbitals give fourteen electron places in an f subshell. So our four capacities are two, six, ten and fourteen. The general count is two l plus one orbitals. For s, p, d and f, l is zero, one, two and three. Now turn each electron place into a column of elements. A quantum count becomes a visible width. Two places make the two-column s block. ## Scene 05 — Building period 1 — 05:30–07:07

They occupy the same spatial orbital with opposite spins. Its configuration is one s squared. Both available spin states are occupied. A third electron cannot join this orbital. No third place is available in that subshell. But where should helium sit? By configuration, it is an s element, with one s squared. By chemistry, it belongs with the noble gases. Its only shell is closed, and removing an electron costs a great deal of energy. So we place it on the far right. The capacities explain the widths; chemistry helps organize the columns. ## Scene 08 — Why a column shares its chemistry — 11:00–12:15

Lithium, sodium and potassium differ in size. Each has a closed core and one outer s electron. That outer electron is the key. Compared with the core electrons, it is much easier to remove. Removing it still costs energy. During a reaction, other energy changes can make the complete process favourable. Each atom can therefore form a positive ion by giving up one electron. We write M becoming M plus and an electron. Three different metals share this common change. For example, each reacts with water to release hydrogen gas. Under comparable conditions, the reaction becomes more vigorous from lithium to sodium to potassium. Now compare the noble gases on the other side of the table. Their closed outer set is n s two, n p six. Removing an electron is difficult, and adding one cannot simply fill a vacancy in that closed set. They are usually unreactive, although heavier noble gases can form compounds. Repeating valence patterns give related chemistry. The same counting idea also explains the long middle rows.

## Scene 13 — Why the rows repeat — 18:36–20:01

## Scene 14 — Why properties change across the table — 20:01–21:28

The same electron structure also helps explain broad trends in properties. Let us start with the size of atoms. Compare lithium through fluorine across period two. Each step adds a proton and an electron. The added electron enters the same principal outer shell. The inner core remains similar, while electrons in that outer shell screen each other incompletely. The effective nuclear attraction generally increases. The electron distribution contracts. The atoms become smaller. Using covalent radii, lithium is a little over twice the size of fluorine. Now compare the alkali metals down group one. A new outer shell opens. More inner electrons shield the nucleus. The atoms grow.
