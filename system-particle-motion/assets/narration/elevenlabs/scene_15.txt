[warm, conversational delivery] Moment of inertia depends on three things: the shape of the body, how its mass is distributed, and which axis it turns about. A thin ring, about its central axis: M R squared. Every bit of its mass is at distance R. A uniform solid disc: one half M R squared. A solid cylinder about its long axis: also one half M R squared. Its length doesn't change the answer. A uniform solid sphere, about a diameter: two fifths M R squared. A thin spherical shell: two thirds M R squared. A thin uniform rod of length L, about its middle: one twelfth M L squared. About one end: one third M L squared. [pause] Line up the round bodies by how far their mass sits from the axis, and the pattern is clear. The more mass near the rim, the larger the moment of inertia. [short pause] [curious] Compare the ring and the disc. With equal mass and radius, the ring's moment of inertia is twice the disc's. Its mass lies farther from the axis, on average. [pause] [warmly] How much mass matters. But where the mass sits can matter just as much.
