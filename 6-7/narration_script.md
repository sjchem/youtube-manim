# Timestamped Narration Script

## 0:00-0:45 - Scene 1: Hook

The "6-7" trend is a viral internet phenomenon. Rooted in the rap song "Doot Doot (6-7)" and NBA star LaMelo Ball's 6'7'' height, it became a massive cultural meme with no set meaning.

But in mathematics, 6 and 7 are not meaningless at all.

They sit side by side like two doors.

One opens into perfect balance.

The other opens into mystery, cycles, and hidden symmetry.

So why does 6 feel so complete, while 7 feels like an outsider?

## 0:45-1:25 - Scene 2: Perfect Six

Start with 6.

Break it into its proper divisors: 1, 2, and 3.

Now let the pieces move back together.

They do not almost make 6. They make exactly 6.

That is what a perfect number is: a number whose smaller divisors add back to the whole.

And 6 has an extra little spark.

The same pieces multiply back to 6 as well.

For the first time, addition and multiplication seem to agree.

## 1:25-2:10 - Scene 3: Hexagon Order

Then 6 steps out of arithmetic and becomes geometry.

A regular hexagon has six equal sides.

Each interior angle is 120 degrees.

Put three of those angles together, and the turn is exactly one full circle.

No gap. No overlap.

That is why hexagons can spread forever across a flat plane.

The honeycomb is not just pretty. It is arithmetic made physical.

## 2:10-2:50 - Scene 4: Prime Outsider

Now move one step to the right.

From 6 to 7.

Suddenly the easy divisions disappear.

Six splits into 2 times 3.

Seven only splits as 1 times 7.

It is prime: whole, indivisible, and a little aloof.

It is also the smallest positive integer that cannot be written as the sum of two squares.

Numbers near it fit into neat square patterns.

Seven slips through.

## 2:50-3:35 - Scene 5: Heptagon Impossibility

The strangeness continues in geometry.

Draw a regular seven-sided polygon.

Its central angle asks for 360 degrees divided by 7.

That division is exact as a number, but it does not belong to the classical compass-and-straightedge world.

A regular heptagon is not constructible by those tools alone.

And unlike the hexagon, its angles do not tile the plane in a clean regular tessellation.

Seven is not broken.

It just refuses the simplest geometric handshake.

## 3:35-4:25 - Scene 6: Decimal Cycle

Then comes the twist.

If you divide 1 by 7, the decimal does not terminate.

But it does not wander randomly either.

Long division creates a loop of remainders.

Each remainder gives the next digit.

And after six steps, the first remainder returns.

The digits are 1, 4, 2, 8, 5, 7.

The outsider has a cycle.

## 4:25-5:15 - Scene 7: Cyclic Number

Now multiply that six-digit block.

Times 1 gives 142857.

Times 2 does not destroy it.

It rotates it.

Times 3 rotates it again.

And this keeps happening through times 6.

The same six digits keep circling, as if the decimal expansion of 1 over 7 had become a tiny clock.

Seven hides order where six displays it openly.

## 5:15-6:05 - Scene 8: Synthesis

So 6 and 7 are not enemies.

They are neighbors showing two moods of mathematics.

Six is balance you can see: divisors, products, hexagons, tilings.

Seven is order you have to chase: primes, impossible constructions, repeating cycles.

Even their connection is elegant.

They are coprime, sharing no factor except 1.

And 7 factorial is 5040, the same number as the minutes in half a week.

Visible order. Hidden order.

That is the beauty of 6 and 7.
